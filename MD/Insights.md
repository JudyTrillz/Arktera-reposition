# Before You Spend More on Marketing, Find Out Where Your Revenue Is Leaking

When a business struggles to generate enough sales, the instinct is often to do more marketing.

Increase the advertising budget. Publish more content. Improve search visibility. Try another platform. Hire someone to generate leads.

Sometimes, these are the right decisions. But they can also be expensive responses to a problem that has not been properly identified.

What if your marketing attracts qualified prospects, but your website fails to convince them to make an inquiry? What if inquiries are coming in, but your team responds too slowly? Or what if sales are happening, but the cost of acquiring and serving clients leaves too little profit?

Each situation requires a different response.

Before investing more in marketing, examine how your business turns attention into revenue, where opportunities may be getting lost, and which problem deserves attention first.

## Why More Marketing May Not Solve the Problem

Marketing is only one part of the process through which a business generates revenue.

A prospect must discover your business, understand your offer, consider you a credible option, take the next step, and eventually decide to buy. After the sale, your pricing, delivery costs, ability to retain clients, and capacity to generate referrals influence the commercial value of that relationship.

A weakness anywhere along this path can limit the return from your wider efforts.

Imagine a consultancy investing in advertising to attract more prospects. The campaign generates inquiries, but the team takes several days to respond. Some prospects move on before a conversation even begins.

Increasing the advertising budget may generate more inquiries, but it will not automatically resolve the response problem. It could simply send more prospects into a process that is already losing opportunities.

The first task is not to assume that marketing is failing. It is to establish what happens after the money is spent.

## What the Research Tells Us

The challenge of generating sales is not limited to one country or industry.

The Federal Reserve Banks' 2026 Report on Employer Firms identified reaching customers and growing sales as the most commonly reported operational challenge among surveyed US small employer firms. Rising costs were the most commonly reported financial challenge.

Separately, Intuit's 2025 Small Business Advertising Trends Report surveyed 1,006 business owners and marketing leaders. Only 25% said they could always measure the return on their advertising spending, while 47% said they could usually measure it.

These findings do not mean businesses should stop advertising, nor do they prove that poor measurement causes weak sales. They do, however, highlight two important questions:

* Are your marketing efforts generating the commercial results your business needs?
* Can you identify which activities contribute to those results?

If you cannot answer these questions with reasonable confidence, increasing spending before investigating performance can make it harder to distinguish a demand problem from a conversion or profitability problem.

## The Six Areas to Examine Before Increasing Your Budget

### 1. Demand: Are You Reaching the Right Prospects?

Marketing activity is not the same as qualified demand.

A campaign may generate impressions, website visits, clicks, or social media engagement without reaching enough people who genuinely need your services, can afford them, or are ready to consider buying.

Start by asking:

* Who is the campaign intended to reach?
* Are the inquiries coming from prospects who fit your target client profile?
* Do prospects understand what you offer?
* Which channels are producing suitable opportunities rather than just activity?

For a dental clinic, a large number of inquiries may have limited value if most are seeking services the clinic does not provide. For a B2B consultancy, website traffic may look encouraging but remain commercially weak if visitors are not decision-makers or potential buyers.

**What to examine:** Qualified inquiries by channel, the proportion of inquiries that fit your target client profile, and the cost of generating those qualified inquiries.

If you are not attracting enough suitable prospects, demand generation may genuinely need improvement. But confirm that the problem is the volume or quality of demand before assuming the budget is too small.

### 2. Trust: Are Prospects Taking the Next Step?

People may visit your website, view your offer, or engage with your content without contacting your business.

The problem may be that prospects do not understand what makes your service relevant to them. They may have unanswered concerns about price, quality, experience, or suitability. They may also encounter a confusing website, an unclear call to action, or an unnecessarily difficult booking or inquiry process.

Ask:

* Do visitors understand what you offer and who it is for?
* Is the next step clear?
* Is there enough relevant information to help them make a decision?
* Can they contact you easily on the devices they use?
* Are meaningful actions being measured accurately?

**What to examine:** The proportion of relevant website visitors who submit an inquiry, book a consultation, request a quote, or complete another meaningful action.

Google Analytics supports tracking important actions, such as lead-form submissions, as key events. This can help businesses move beyond counting visits to measuring actions that matter to their objectives.

However, a low website conversion rate does not automatically prove that the website design is the problem. Traffic quality, offer relevance, pricing, and market conditions may also influence results.

**The priority is to identify what prevents suitable prospects from taking the next step, rather than redesigning the website or increasing traffic by default.**

### 3. Conversion: Are Inquiries Turning into Clients?

Generating an inquiry is not the same as winning a client.

Once someone contacts your business, the quality of the subsequent process matters. Prospects may receive delayed responses, inconsistent follow-up, unclear explanations, or no obvious next step.

Some may be unsuitable prospects. Others may be interested but need more information before making a decision.

Review what happens after each inquiry:

* How long does it take to respond?
* Is responsibility for responding clearly assigned?
* Are inquiries qualified consistently?
* Does each suitable prospect have a clear next step?
* Are follow-up activities recorded and completed?
* Do you know why opportunities are won or lost?

**What to examine:** Response time, the proportion of inquiries successfully contacted, the proportion of qualified inquiries that progress to a booking or sales conversation, and the proportion that become paying clients.

These measures help distinguish between a shortage of inquiries and a problem converting existing opportunities.

If a business receives enough qualified inquiries but few become sales, buying more leads may be premature. The business should first investigate the reasons for the low conversion rate.

That does not mean every lost sale can be recovered. Some prospects will never be a suitable fit, and others will choose a competitor for legitimate reasons. The objective is to identify avoidable losses, not to assume every lost opportunity was recoverable.

### 4. Delivery: Are Your Sales Producing Healthy Margins?

A business can increase sales without improving its financial position.

Consider a service provider that attracts more clients by offering substantial discounts. Sales volume rises, but the additional delivery work, staff time, software, subcontracting, and other costs consume much of the additional revenue.

The same problem can arise when a business acquires clients at a high cost but earns too little from each engagement to justify that expense.

Investigate:

* How much revenue does a typical engagement generate?
* What direct costs are required to deliver it?
* How much does it cost to acquire a client, where this can be measured reliably?
* How much staff time does each engagement require?
* Which services or client groups are yielding the healthiest margins?
* Are capacity constraints or inefficient processes increasing delivery costs?

**What to examine:** Revenue per engagement, contribution margin, customer acquisition cost where it can be measured reliably, estimated versus actual delivery hours, and the resources required to deliver each service.

Be careful not to confuse revenue with profit or cash flow. They answer different questions. A sale may be recorded as revenue before payment is collected, and a high-revenue engagement may still be commercially unattractive if its costs are excessive.

If the real constraint is weak pricing or inefficient delivery, increasing marketing activity could bring in more work without solving the financial problem.

### 5. Retention: Are Clients Returning?

Winning a client is not necessarily the end of the commercial relationship.

Depending on the service and business model, clients may need ongoing support, periodic maintenance, additional engagements, follow-up appointments, or other services over time. If the relationship ends immediately after the first transaction, the business may be missing legitimate opportunities to serve those clients again.

Investigate:

* Do clients return when they have a relevant need?
* Is there a consistent process for post-service communication?
* Are clients satisfied with the experience they receive?
* Are follow-up responsibilities clearly assigned?
* Do you understand why clients return, discontinue service, or choose another provider?

**What to examine:** Repeat-purchase rate, returning-client revenue, retention or churn for recurring-service models, and feedback or follow-up completion rates where relevant.

The appropriate measure depends on the business. A subscription company may monitor churn monthly, while a specialist consultancy may evaluate repeat engagements over a longer period. A one-time service may have limited natural repeat-purchase potential.

The objective is not to force repeat purchases where they do not make sense. It is to understand whether the business is maintaining valuable client relationships and providing appropriate opportunities for continued service.

### 6. Referral: Are Satisfied Clients Generating New Opportunities?

Satisfied clients can become a source of new business when they recommend your services, introduce suitable prospects, or share their experiences with others.

But satisfaction does not automatically produce referrals. Clients may be happy with your work without thinking to recommend you, knowing how to do so, or recognizing when someone in their network needs your services.

Consider:

* Do satisfied clients recommend your business?
* Do you make it easy for clients to leave honest reviews or share feedback?
* Is there an appropriate process for requesting introductions or recommendations?
* Do professional relationships or partnerships generate qualified opportunities?
* Can you identify which new opportunities originate from referrals?

**What to examine:** The number of qualified referral inquiries, the proportion of new clients acquired through referrals, referral-to-client conversion, and revenue attributable to referral opportunities where tracking is practical.

Referral activity should be evaluated in context. Some service businesses depend heavily on personal recommendations, while others rely more on search, partnerships, outbound sales, or paid acquisition. A low referral rate is not automatically a problem if other channels are performing effectively.

The key is to determine whether referrals represent a realistic growth opportunity for your business and whether satisfied clients have a clear, appropriate way to recommend your services.

## A Simple Example: Where Might the Revenue Be Getting Lost?

Consider a hypothetical service business that receives 100 inquiries in a month.

Of those inquiries, 60 become qualified sales opportunities. Fifteen of those opportunities become paying clients.

Its inquiry-to-client conversion rate is therefore 15%.

Now suppose the business investigates its records and discovers that some suitable prospects are not being contacted promptly, while others receive no consistent follow-up. The business decides to improve response handling and track the outcome over the following months.

The goal is to determine whether these changes improve conversion, not to assume they will.

For illustration, if the business eventually converts 18 of every 100 comparable inquiries, its inquiry-to-client conversion rate would rise from 15% to 18%, a three-percentage-point increase.

That is a relative increase of 20% in the conversion rate, but it is not automatically a 20% increase in revenue or profit. The financial result depends on customer value, delivery costs, capacity, and whether the inquiries are comparable in quality.

The example shows why measuring the existing process matters. Before paying for more inquiries, a business may learn something valuable by understanding what happens to the opportunities it already receives.

These figures are illustrative, not industry benchmarks or a prediction of what your business should achieve.

## How to Decide What to Fix First

Once you have examined the six areas, resist the temptation to fix everything at once.

For each suspected problem, consider four questions.

**1. How much commercial value could be affected?**

Estimate the revenue, costs, time, or opportunities associated with the problem. Use your own business data where possible.

**2. How strong is the evidence?**

Distinguish a measured problem from an assumption. If the data is incomplete, identify what you need to measure before making a larger investment.

**3. How practical is the intervention?**

Consider implementation costs, staff time, available resources, and operational capacity.

**4. How will you know whether the change worked?**

Choose a baseline measure and define what you will monitor after the change. Avoid relying on a single metric if it could improve while the wider business outcome worsens.

A useful starting point is to prioritize problems that have meaningful commercial consequences, reasonable evidence behind them, and a practical path to investigation or improvement.

The highest-priority problem is not always the easiest one to fix. But it should be a problem worth understanding before committing more resources.

## So, Should You Increase Your Marketing Budget?

Sometimes, yes.

If your business is reaching the right audience, converting suitable prospects effectively, delivering its services profitably, and measuring results with reasonable confidence, additional investment may be justified.

But if you do not know whether the main constraint is demand, trust, conversion, delivery, retention, or referrals, increasing spending is a decision made with incomplete information.

The better approach is to establish a baseline, examine the journey from marketing activity to commercial results, and investigate the most consequential gaps.

That gives you a stronger basis for deciding whether to spend more, improve an existing process, change your offer, strengthen follow-up, improve delivery efficiency, or address a problem elsewhere in the business.

## Find the Problem Before You Fund the Next Fix

Marketing is an important part of business growth, but it cannot compensate for every weakness in the wider revenue system.

The aim is not to reduce spending automatically or assume your marketing is the problem. It is to understand what your business needs before deciding where to invest next.

# Getting Website Traffic but No Inquiries? How to Find the Conversion Problem

Your website is getting visitors. Your analytics show people arriving from search engines, social media, advertising, or other sources.

But the inquiries aren't coming in.

Few people complete your contact form. Consultation bookings remain low. Quote requests are inconsistent. And despite investing time or money into attracting visitors, your website produces little evidence of new business opportunities.

The obvious response is to get more traffic.

But what if the problem isn't the number of people visiting your website? What if the right people are arriving but cannot find a compelling reason to contact you, don't trust what they see, or encounter unnecessary friction when trying to take the next step?

Before investing in more traffic or commissioning a website redesign, investigate how your website turns visits into meaningful business opportunities.

## Traffic Is an Activity. Inquiries Are an Outcome.

Website traffic tells you that people are visiting. It doesn't tell you whether those visitors are suitable prospects, understand your offer, trust your business, or intend to buy.

Imagine a consultancy attracting 2,000 website visits a month but receiving very few qualified inquiries.

There are several possible explanations.

The website may attract people looking for free information rather than professional services. Its messaging may describe what the consultancy does without explaining why a prospective client should care. Visitors may be uncertain about the firm's experience or suitability. Alternatively, the website may make it difficult to book a consultation or submit an inquiry.

Each possibility points to a different investigation.

Buying more traffic before understanding the problem could increase website visits without producing a meaningful improvement in commercial results.

**The first question isn't how to attract more visitors. It's why the visitors you already attract aren't taking the next step.**

## What Should a Business Measure?

Google Analytics supports measuring meaningful actions, such as lead-form submissions, as key events. This allows a business to examine actions that matter to its objectives rather than relying exclusively on visitor counts.

For a service business, relevant actions might include:

* Submitting an inquiry form.
* Booking a consultation.
* Requesting a quote.
* Calling the business from the website, where call tracking is available.
* Starting a relevant conversation through an appropriate contact channel.

The right measurement depends on how customers engage with the business.

A dental clinic may care about appointment requests. A consultancy may measure qualified discovery-call bookings. A contractor may focus on quote requests that lead to suitable projects.

These actions are not equally valuable, and not every inquiry becomes a sale. Where practical, track what happens after the initial conversion so you can distinguish raw inquiries from qualified opportunities and paying clients.

Without that context, a website can appear successful because it generates many form submissions even when few of those inquiries are relevant.

## Five Reasons Your Website May Be Getting Traffic but Few Inquiries

### 1. You're Attracting the Wrong Visitors

A website can attract substantial traffic without reaching enough people who need the services it offers.

For example, a consultancy publishing general educational content may attract students, job seekers, and people looking for free templates. Those visitors may be valuable to its audience, but they may not be prospective clients.

Likewise, an advertising campaign can generate clicks from people who are curious about an offer but have little intention or ability to purchase it.

Before changing the website, examine where visitors come from and what they appear to be looking for.

Ask:

* Which channels and search queries bring visitors to the website?
* Are those visitors in the markets and locations the business serves?
* Does the landing page match the message or promise that attracted them?
* Which sources generate meaningful inquiries rather than just visits?
* Are you attracting people who need the service and are suitable prospects?

**What to examine:** Traffic by channel, landing-page performance, relevant search queries, location data where appropriate, and inquiry rates by traffic source.

If the wrong people are arriving, the solution may involve better targeting, more relevant content, changes to campaign messaging, or a clearer definition of the intended audience.

A website redesign may do little to solve a problem that begins before visitors reach the page.

### 2. Your Offer Is Unclear or Unconvincing

Visitors need to understand what your business offers, who it helps, and why the service may be relevant to them.

Yet some websites focus heavily on describing the company, listing services, or presenting technical capabilities without clearly explaining the value to the buyer.

Consider the difference between these messages:

*We provide integrated digital solutions for growing businesses.*

And:

*We help service businesses identify what's preventing website inquiries from turning into sales and determine what to improve next.*

The first describes a broad capability. The second communicates a more specific problem and the outcome the business is trying to support.

An offer can also be unclear because it tries to appeal to too many audiences at once, uses unfamiliar language, or asks visitors to take an action before they understand the value of doing so.

Ask:

* Can a first-time visitor understand what the business does?
* Is it clear who the service is intended for?
* Does the messaging address a problem the visitor recognizes?
* Is the value of the service explained in terms the buyer understands?
* Does the page give visitors enough information to judge whether the service is suitable?

**What to examine:** Landing-page messaging, headline clarity, alignment between the offer and the intended audience, and the actions visitors take after arriving on the page.

If the offer is unclear, improve the message before assuming you need a different website design.

### 3. Visitors Don't Have Enough Reason to Trust You

Even when visitors understand your offer, they may hesitate to make contact.

Hiring a service provider can involve financial risk, uncertainty about quality, and concerns about whether the provider can deliver what it promises.

A website that makes broad claims without supporting evidence may leave visitors with unanswered questions.

Depending on the business, useful trust signals may include:

* Relevant case studies.
* Specific examples of work completed.
* Genuine client testimonials or reviews.
* Clear explanations of the process.
* Relevant qualifications, experience, or credentials.
* Transparent descriptions of services and expectations.
* Answers to common questions about price, suitability, or delivery.

The right evidence depends on what the buyer needs to know. A professional consultancy may need to demonstrate relevant expertise and explain how engagements work. A local service provider may benefit from genuine customer reviews, clear service-area information, and evidence of completed work.

Trust signals should be accurate and verifiable. Invented testimonials, unsupported performance claims, or irrelevant badges can undermine credibility rather than strengthen it.

Ask:

* What concerns might prevent a suitable prospect from making contact?
* Does the website answer those concerns?
* Is the evidence relevant to the service being offered?
* Can visitors verify important claims?
* Are the business's limitations and expectations communicated clearly?

**What to examine:** The proof presented on important landing pages, the questions visitors ask before buying, and the points where suitable prospects disengage.

If visitors cannot establish whether your business is credible and suitable, sending more of them to the same page may not resolve the problem.

### 4. The Website Makes Taking Action Too Difficult

Sometimes, visitors understand the offer and trust the business but still fail to make an inquiry because the next step is inconvenient or confusing.

The contact button may be difficult to find. The form may ask for unnecessary information. The booking process may be unclear. A mobile visitor may struggle to use the page. Or a form may fail without making the problem obvious.

These issues can be easy to overlook because the website may appear to work perfectly when viewed by its owner.

Test the experience as a prospective client would.

Ask:

* Is the primary action easy to identify?
* Does the button explain what happens next?
* Can visitors complete the form comfortably on a mobile device?
* Are the requested details necessary at this stage?
* Do form errors explain how to correct the problem?
* Does the confirmation message tell visitors what happens after submission?
* Can someone contact the business through an alternative appropriate channel?

**What to examine:** Form-start and form-completion rates where measurable, errors during submission, mobile usability, booking completion, and whether the website reliably records successful inquiries.

Do not assume that every abandoned form is caused by poor design. Some visitors change their minds, discover the service isn't suitable, or decide they aren't ready to proceed. The goal is to identify avoidable friction and determine whether it materially affects the business.

The priority is to make the next step clear, relevant, and reasonably easy to complete.

### 5. You're Not Measuring the Right Things

Sometimes, the problem is not the website itself. It's that the business cannot reliably determine what visitors are doing.

A website may record page views but fail to track successful form submissions. A click on a contact button may be counted as a conversion even when the visitor never completes the inquiry. Alternatively, inquiries may arrive through calls, email, or messaging channels that are not connected to the website's reporting.

In these circumstances, decisions are being made using incomplete information.

Start by defining what counts as a meaningful conversion for your business.

Then verify that the relevant action is measured correctly. If submitting a contact form is important, test the entire process and confirm that successful submissions appear in your analytics. If prospects use several contact channels, establish a practical way to record those inquiries and connect them to subsequent outcomes where possible.

Ask:

* Are successful inquiries recorded accurately?
* Are clicks being confused with completed actions?
* Can you distinguish qualified inquiries from irrelevant submissions?
* Can you identify the source of an inquiry when the available data permits?
* Do you know which inquiries eventually become clients?

**What to examine:** Analytics event configuration, successful form submissions, conversion definitions, inquiry records, and the connection between marketing sources and business outcomes.

Accurate measurement will not automatically improve conversion. It gives you a more reliable basis for identifying the problem and deciding what to test next.

## A Practical Example: Finding the Weak Point

Consider a hypothetical professional services website that receives 1,500 visits in a month and generates 12 inquiries.

Its visitor-to-inquiry rate is:

**12 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = 0.8%**

That figure alone does not tell us whether the website is performing poorly.

We would need to understand the source and relevance of the traffic, the type of service being offered, the intended audience, the conversion action being measured, and how the website contributes to the wider sales process.

Suppose further investigation shows that a large share of visits comes from people searching for information unrelated to the firm's paid services.

In that case, improving audience targeting and aligning landing-page content with the intended buyer may deserve attention before redesigning the contact form.

Now imagine the opposite situation: the website attracts relevant visitors, its offer is clear, and visitors engage with the service pages, but many start an inquiry form and fail to complete it.

That would justify a closer examination of the form, the information requested, technical errors, and the mobile experience.

The same headline metric can point to very different problems.

For illustration, if the website later generated 18 inquiries from the same 1,500 visits, the visitor-to-inquiry rate would rise to 1.2%. That is an increase of 0.4 percentage points, or 50% relative to the original rate.

However, this would not automatically mean revenue had increased by 50%. The commercial impact would depend on the quality of those inquiries, the proportion that became clients, the value of the engagements, and the cost of delivering the services.

These figures are illustrative, not industry benchmarks or expected results.

## How to Decide What to Fix First

Once you've identified possible problems, avoid changing the entire website at once.

Use a simple process.

**1. Establish the baseline.**

Record relevant traffic, inquiries, qualified inquiries, and subsequent sales where the data is available. Check that the measures are defined consistently.

**2. Identify the strongest evidence of a problem.**

Look for patterns in traffic sources, landing-page performance, user behaviour, inquiry records, or feedback from prospects. Treat unexplained gaps as questions to investigate, not proof of a particular cause.

**3. Choose a targeted intervention.**

Depending on the evidence, this might involve adjusting audience targeting, clarifying the offer, adding relevant proof, simplifying a form, improving mobile usability, or correcting analytics.

**4. Measure what happens next.**

Compare results against the baseline over a suitable period. Account for changes in traffic quality, seasonality, campaign mix, and other factors that could affect the outcome.

**5. Refine the approach.**

If the change doesn't improve the intended measure, investigate why. The original explanation may have been incomplete, the intervention may have been ineffective, or another constraint may be limiting results.

The goal is not to make the website look better by default. It is to improve its ability to help suitable prospects understand the offer, take the next step, and enter a sales process that can serve them effectively.

## When Should You Redesign the Website?

A redesign may be appropriate when the existing website has substantial usability limitations, cannot support the business's requirements, creates persistent technical problems, or no longer represents the offer accurately.

But redesigning the website should not be the automatic response to low inquiry volume.

If the main issue is irrelevant traffic, the business may need to improve targeting. If visitors do not understand the offer, messaging may need attention. If inquiries are lost after submission, the sales process may be the constraint. And if conversions are not being measured correctly, the first task is to fix the measurement system.

A new design cannot automatically solve problems that originate elsewhere.

**Identify the constraint first. Then decide whether the website needs a targeted improvement, a broader redesign, or no major change at all.**

## Turn Website Traffic into Better Business Decisions

A website should do more than attract visitors. It should help suitable prospects understand your offer, establish confidence in your business, and take an appropriate next step.

When inquiries are low, the answer is not always more traffic, more content, or a new design.

The important questions are whether the right people are visiting, whether the offer gives them a reason to act, whether the experience supports that action, and whether the business can measure what happens afterward.

# Why Businesses Lose Sales After the First Inquiry

A potential client contacts your business. Your team receives the inquiry, perhaps sends an initial response, and waits.

The prospect doesn't reply.

A few days pass. Someone intends to follow up, but another task takes priority. The inquiry remains in an inbox, a spreadsheet, or a CRM record without a clear next action.

Eventually, the team assumes the prospect wasn't interested and moves on.

Meanwhile, the business continues investing in marketing to generate new inquiries.

This scenario doesn't happen in every business, and not every silent prospect would have become a client. But it raises an important question: **are you losing sales because you lack demand, or because your business isn't consistently managing the opportunities it already receives?**

Before spending more to attract prospects, examine what happens between the first inquiry and the final buying decision.

## The Follow-Up Problem Is Bigger Than Response Time

Responding promptly matters, but sales conversion involves more than sending the first reply quickly.

A prospect may need clarification about the service, reassurance about the provider's suitability, a quotation, an appointment, or time to discuss the decision internally. The appropriate next step depends on the prospect's needs and the complexity of the purchase.

A business can respond quickly and still lose the opportunity if it fails to qualify the inquiry, address relevant concerns, or follow through on what it promised.

Likewise, a prospect who doesn't respond immediately isn't necessarily lost. They may be busy, comparing options, waiting for approval, or reconsidering their priorities.

The objective is not to pressure every prospect into buying. It is to make sure suitable opportunities receive a timely, relevant, and well-managed sales process.

## What the Research Tells Us

Research suggests that businesses should take lead handling seriously, while also recognizing the limits of the available evidence.

In a 2011 Harvard Business Review study, researchers examined how 2,241 US companies handled online sales inquiries. Only 37% responded within an hour, and 23% never responded. Among companies that responded within 30 days, the average response time was 42 hours.

The study is historical, so these figures should not be interpreted as today's response rates. However, the research illustrates a basic operational failure: an inquiry can create a sales opportunity, but that opportunity has little chance of progressing if the business never responds.

A more recent signal comes from Zapier's 2026 survey of more than 400 US B2B sales and marketing managers. The company reported that 92% of respondents said their teams regularly lost qualified leads because follow-ups were delayed, inconsistent, or forgotten.

This was a survey of managers' reported experiences, not an independent audit of every lead or a measurement of the proportion of leads that could have been converted. The finding should therefore be treated as evidence of a reported business problem, not proof that 92% of sales opportunities are recoverable.

Taken together, the studies point to a practical question:

**Does your business have a reliable process for handling inquiries from the moment they arrive until they are won, lost, or deliberately placed on hold?**

## Five Places Where Sales Opportunities Can Break Down

### 1. The Inquiry Is Never Properly Captured

Before a business can follow up, it must know that an inquiry exists.

Prospects may contact a company through website forms, telephone calls, email, social media, messaging apps, referrals, or other channels. If those inquiries are scattered across different inboxes and devices, some may never enter the sales process.

For example, a contractor might record website inquiries in a spreadsheet while calls go directly to a project manager's phone. When the manager becomes busy, a request for a quotation can remain unnoticed.

The problem isn't necessarily a shortage of leads. It may be that the business lacks a reliable way to capture and assign them.

Ask:

* Which channels can prospects use to contact the business?
* Are inquiries recorded in a consistent place?
* Who is responsible for checking each channel?
* How are inquiries assigned when the usual contact person is unavailable?
* Can the business identify inquiries that received no response?

**What to examine:** Total inquiries received by channel, the proportion recorded in the sales system, ownership of each inquiry, and the number of inquiries with no documented response.

If inquiries are going missing, adding more lead-generation activity could increase the volume of opportunities the business fails to handle.

### 2. The First Response Is Too Slow or Unclear

A prospect who has made contact should not have to wonder whether the business received the inquiry or what happens next.

Response time can matter, particularly when the prospect is comparing providers or needs help with an immediate problem. But the right response standard depends on the service, the channel, the prospect's expectations, and the business's operating hours.

A response doesn't always need to be a complete sales conversation. Sometimes the immediate goal is to acknowledge the inquiry, confirm the next step, and set a realistic expectation for when the prospect will receive a fuller answer.

Ask:

* How long does the business take to acknowledge new inquiries?
* Does response time vary by channel or time of day?
* Does the first response answer the prospect's immediate question?
* Does it explain what happens next?
* Is there a clear owner responsible for responding?

**What to examine:** Median and range of first-response times, the proportion of inquiries answered within the business's defined service standard, and whether response performance differs by channel.

Avoid adopting a universal response-time target simply because it appears in a sales article. Establish a realistic standard based on the business's customer expectations, resources, and buying process, then measure whether the team meets it.

### 3. The Business Doesn't Qualify the Inquiry Properly

Not every inquiry is a suitable sales opportunity.

Some prospects may need a service the business doesn't provide. Others may have a budget, timeline, location, or set of requirements that doesn't fit the business's capabilities.

Without a consistent qualification process, the team can waste time pursuing unsuitable inquiries while failing to give suitable prospects the attention they need.

Qualification should not become an excuse to dismiss prospects too quickly. Its purpose is to understand the prospect's needs and determine whether the business can genuinely help.

Depending on the service, relevant questions may include:

* What problem is the prospect trying to solve?
* Does the requested service match what the business provides?
* Is the prospect in the service area or target market?
* What timing or constraints matter to the prospect?
* What information is needed to recommend the appropriate next step?

**What to examine:** The proportion of inquiries that meet defined qualification criteria, the reasons inquiries are deemed unsuitable, and conversion rates among qualified opportunities.

If a high volume of inquiries is unsuitable, the underlying issue may begin with targeting, messaging, or the offer itself. If inquiries are suitable but rarely progress, the next stage of the sales process deserves closer examination.

### 4. Follow-Up Stops Before the Decision

A prospect may not be ready to buy after the first conversation.

They may need to compare proposals, discuss the purchase with a colleague, secure a budget, review contract terms, or understand how the service would work.

Silence does not reveal which explanation applies.

Some prospects will have decided against the offer. Others may still be evaluating it. Without an appropriate follow-up process, the business may never learn the difference.

Consider a consultancy that sends a proposal and waits for the prospect to reply. The team assumes that no response means the prospect has declined. Yet the prospect may have unanswered questions about scope, timing, or expected outcomes.

A useful follow-up should have a reason. It might clarify an unresolved question, confirm whether the proposal meets the prospect's requirements, or establish whether the project remains a priority.

Ask:

* Does every active opportunity have a documented next step?
* Is responsibility for follow-up assigned?
* Are proposals followed up appropriately?
* Does follow-up address the prospect's actual situation?
* Is there a process for recording the prospect's decision or reason for not proceeding?

**What to examine:** The proportion of active opportunities with a scheduled next action, follow-up completion, time between sales stages, and conversion rates for opportunities with different follow-up patterns.

The aim is not to contact prospects endlessly. It is to avoid allowing legitimate opportunities to disappear simply because nobody owns the next step.

Follow-up should also respect the prospect's preferences, applicable privacy requirements, and requests not to be contacted.

### 5. The Business Doesn't Learn Why Opportunities Are Won or Lost

When a prospect doesn't buy, the team may record the opportunity as lost without understanding why.

The assumed reason might be price, a competitor, timing, or a lack of interest. But unless the reason is supported by what the prospect said or what the team observed, it remains an assumption.

That creates a problem for decision-making. If the business assumes price is the main obstacle, it may reduce prices when the real issue is unclear scope or weak communication. If it assumes lead quality is poor, it may increase marketing spending without investigating how the team handles suitable inquiries.

Ask:

* Are won and lost opportunities recorded consistently?
* Are reasons for loss based on evidence or assumptions?
* At which stage do opportunities most often stop progressing?
* Do patterns differ by service, source, or prospect type?
* Are lessons from lost opportunities used to improve the process?

**What to examine:** Conversion between sales stages, time spent at each stage, documented reasons for lost opportunities, and differences in outcomes across lead sources or service types.

Do not expect every prospect to explain why they chose another provider. The goal is to improve the quality of the evidence available, not to force certainty where none exists.

Over time, consistent records can help the business identify patterns and determine which parts of its sales process deserve attention.

## A Practical Example: What Is Happening to Your Inquiries?

Consider a hypothetical service business that receives 80 inquiries in a month.

Of those inquiries:

* 60 receive a documented response.
* 40 are qualified as suitable opportunities.
* 12 become paying clients.

The business converts 12 of its 80 inquiries into clients, giving it an inquiry-to-client conversion rate of 15%.

But that single figure does not reveal where the main problem lies.

The 20 inquiries without a documented response may indicate a capture or response-handling problem. The 20 inquiries that were contacted but did not qualify may point to audience, offer, or targeting issues. The 28 qualified opportunities that did not become clients may require a closer examination of sales conversations, follow-up, timing, pricing, or other factors.

These are starting points for investigation, not conclusions. The business would need more information to establish why each opportunity failed to progress.

Suppose the company improves its inquiry handling and, over a comparable period, converts 15 of every 80 inquiries into clients. Its conversion rate would rise from 15% to 18.75%.

That is an increase of 3.75 percentage points, or 25% relative to the original conversion rate.

It would not automatically represent a 25% increase in revenue or profit. The commercial result would depend on client value, delivery costs, capacity, and whether the inquiries were comparable in quality.

All figures in this example are illustrative, not industry benchmarks or predictions.

The practical lesson is that a business needs to understand how opportunities move through its sales process before deciding whether the priority is more demand, better qualification, faster response, stronger follow-up, or something else.

## Build a Follow-Up Process That People Can Actually Use

A reliable process does not have to begin with expensive software.

It needs clear ownership, consistent records, and a defined next step for each active opportunity.

A practical starting point is to establish the following:

**1. Record every inquiry.**
Choose a central place to capture inquiries from the channels the business uses. This could be a well-maintained spreadsheet or a CRM, depending on the complexity and volume of the sales process.

**2. Assign responsibility.**
Make it clear who responds to each inquiry and who takes over when that person is unavailable.

**3. Define the next step.**
After the first response, record what needs to happen next, who owns it, and when it should happen.

**4. Use relevant follow-up.**
Follow up according to the prospect's needs and the buying process. A complex B2B engagement may require a different approach from a request for an appointment or a straightforward local service quote.

**5. Record the outcome.**
Track whether the opportunity progresses, is won, is lost, remains open, or is unsuitable. Where possible, record the reason using consistent categories.

**6. Review the process regularly.**
Look for inquiries without responses, overdue next steps, stalled opportunities, and recurring reasons for loss. Use those patterns to decide what to improve.

Automation may help when inquiries arrive through multiple channels, follow-up tasks are frequently missed, or manual administration is consuming too much time. But technology should support a workable process, not replace the need to define one.

A poorly designed process can still fail after being automated.

## How to Decide What to Fix First

If the business is experiencing several of these problems, avoid trying to change everything at once.

Start by identifying where the strongest evidence of a breakdown exists.

If inquiries are not consistently recorded, fix capture and ownership first. If response times are poor, investigate workload, routing, and responsibility. If many inquiries are unsuitable, review targeting and the offer. If qualified opportunities stall after proposals are sent, examine the follow-up and decision process.

For each proposed change, establish a baseline and choose a measure that reflects the intended outcome.

For example:

| Suspected problem               | Measure to establish                        | Potential area to investigate            |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Inquiries go unrecorded         | Percentage of inquiries captured            | Channel monitoring and inquiry routing   |
| Responses are delayed           | Median first-response time                  | Ownership, workload, and notifications   |
| Many inquiries are unsuitable   | Qualification rate                          | Targeting, messaging, and offer fit      |
| Qualified opportunities stall   | Stage-to-stage conversion and time in stage | Sales process, objections, and follow-up |
| Lost opportunities teach little | Completeness of loss reasons                | Sales records and review practices       |

These measures help the business investigate rather than guess. They do not, on their own, prove causation or guarantee that a proposed change will improve results.

## Should You Buy More Leads?

Sometimes, generating more leads is exactly what a business needs.

If the business has a functioning inquiry process, responds consistently, follows up appropriately, and converts suitable opportunities at an acceptable rate, increasing demand may be a reasonable next step.

But if inquiries are being missed, responses are delayed, or qualified prospects receive no clear next step, additional lead generation may increase the amount of demand the business struggles to manage.

The decision should be based on the evidence.

First, understand how inquiries are captured and handled. Then identify where suitable opportunities stop progressing. Finally, determine whether the business needs more demand, a better sales process, improved follow-up, or a combination of changes.

## Turn More of Your Existing Opportunities into Better Decisions

A business does not need to convert every inquiry to perform well. Some prospects will be unsuitable, some will choose another provider, and some will decide not to buy.

The goal is to avoid preventable losses and understand the factors that influence sales performance.

That requires more than a lead count. It requires a clear view of what happens from the first inquiry through qualification, follow-up, and the final decision.

Revenue growth doesn't always mean healthier profits. Learn how to examine margins, pricing, delivery costs, and operating expenses to understand why a business may be selling more without keeping more.

# Revenue Is Growing, but Profit Isn't: What Should You Investigate?

More customers are buying. Sales are increasing. The business appears to be moving forward.

But when you review the financial results, profit has barely changed. In some cases, it has fallen.

This creates a frustrating situation for a business owner. You are doing more work, serving more customers, and generating more revenue, yet the financial reward does not seem to reflect the effort.

The instinct may be to attract even more customers. More sales should eventually solve the problem, right?

Not necessarily.

If each additional sale contributes less profit than expected, or the business incurs extra costs to deliver the work, increasing sales may add pressure without resolving the underlying problem.

**Before you focus on growing revenue further, investigate what is happening to the money the business earns and spends.**

*Currency note: Naira equivalents in the examples use an illustrative reference rate of US$1 = ₦1,350, dated 14 October 2026. Exchange rates vary by source, transaction type, and date. These conversions are for illustration, not guaranteed transaction rates.*

## Revenue Growth and Profit Growth Are Different

Revenue is the money a business earns from selling its products or services before expenses are deducted.

Profit is what remains after the relevant costs and expenses have been accounted for.

The distinction matters because revenue can rise while costs rise faster.

Imagine a service business that earns **US$7,407 (₦10 million)** in monthly revenue and retains **US$1,481 (₦2 million)** as operating profit. Its operating profit margin is approximately 20%.

A few months later, monthly revenue reaches **US$8,889 (₦12 million)**, but operating profit remains at **US$1,481 (₦2 million)**. Revenue has increased by 20%, yet the operating profit margin has fallen to approximately 16.7%.

The business is selling more, but it is retaining a smaller share of its revenue as operating profit.

These figures are illustrative, not an industry benchmark. They demonstrate why revenue alone is not enough to evaluate business performance.

The US Federal Reserve Banks' 2026 Report on Employer Firms found that rising costs of goods, services, and wages were the most commonly reported financial challenge among surveyed small employer firms. The survey covered US businesses, so its findings should not be treated as a universal measure. However, it reinforces the importance of examining costs alongside sales growth.

## Five Areas to Investigate When Revenue Grows but Profit Doesn't

### 1. Are Your Direct Costs Growing Faster Than Sales?

Start with the costs directly associated with delivering what you sell.

Depending on the business, these may include:

* Materials, stock, or supplies
* Contractor payments
* Staff time spent delivering a service
* Transaction or fulfilment fees
* Transport and other delivery expenses
* Rework required to correct mistakes

A business may increase its prices or sell more services while experiencing a disproportionate increase in the cost of fulfilling those sales.

For example, a consulting firm may win more projects but underestimate the hours required to complete them. A contractor may secure more jobs while spending more than expected on materials, transport, and additional labour.

In both cases, sales can rise without a corresponding improvement in the financial return.

**What to examine:**

Compare revenue and direct delivery costs over the same periods. Where possible, calculate the gross profit and gross profit margin for individual services or product categories.

Gross profit is generally calculated as:

**Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of goods sold or direct costs**

**Gross profit margin = Gross profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

The exact classification of direct costs depends on the business and its accounting method. Use consistent definitions when comparing periods.

If gross profit margin is declining, investigate which costs have increased, which services contribute less, and whether the prices charged still reflect the resources required to deliver the work.

### 2. Are Your Prices Still Appropriate?

A price that was profitable when you introduced a service may no longer be profitable after your costs change.

The problem may also be the way the service is priced.

For example, a business that charges a fixed fee for a project may discover that the work regularly takes twice as many hours as anticipated. The quoted price has not changed, but the actual cost of delivery has.

Discounts can create a similar problem. They may help secure a sale, but they also reduce the revenue available to cover the cost of fulfilling that sale.

Consider a service sold for **US$148 (₦200,000)** with direct delivery costs of **US$89 (₦120,000)**. Before other expenses, it contributes approximately **US$59 (₦80,000)**.

If the business discounts the service to **US$133 (₦180,000)** while its delivery costs remain **US$89 (₦120,000)**, that contribution falls to approximately **US$44 (₦60,000)**.

The price has decreased by 10%, but the contribution from the sale has decreased by 25%.

These are illustrative figures, rounded to whole dollars. The naira equivalents use the stated reference rate.

**What to examine:**

* Actual delivery cost compared with the original estimate
* Discounts and price adjustments
* Profit contribution by service or product
* Time required to complete each type of engagement
* Whether complex work is being priced appropriately
* Whether the business is absorbing additional work outside the agreed scope

Do not conclude immediately that prices must increase. First establish whether the underlying issue is pricing, delivery efficiency, service scope, or a combination of factors.

### 3. Are Operating Expenses Consuming the Additional Revenue?

Even if the business maintains a healthy gross profit margin, its operating profit may decline when overhead increases.

Operating expenses can include rent, administrative salaries, software subscriptions, utilities, insurance, professional fees, and other costs required to run the business.

Some expenses rise gradually. Others increase when a business expands.

A growing service business may hire additional staff, move into a larger office, purchase new software, or add management layers to support higher demand. These decisions may be justified, but their financial effects need to be measured.

The US Small Business Administration's break-even guidance distinguishes fixed costs from variable costs and recommends accounting for the expenses required to operate a business. It also notes that some costs have both fixed and variable components.

**What to examine:**

* Which expenses have increased since revenue began growing?
* Were the additional costs necessary to generate the extra revenue?
* Are new subscriptions, tools, or contractors being used effectively?
* Are staffing and administrative costs increasing faster than the work they support?
* Are quarterly or annual expenses being compared consistently across periods?

The goal is not to cut every expense that has increased. It is to determine which expenses support profitable growth and which require closer scrutiny.

Cutting a cost that protects service quality or enables future growth may create a different problem. Decisions should be based on the purpose and financial effect of each expense.

### 4. Are You Selling More of the Less Profitable Work?

Total revenue can hide significant differences in profitability across services, products, and customer groups.

Imagine a business that sells two services:

* **Service A:** US$370 (₦500,000) in revenue with US$222 (₦300,000) in direct costs.
* **Service B:** US$370 (₦500,000) in revenue with US$296 (₦400,000) in direct costs.

Service A contributes approximately **US$148 (₦200,000)** before overhead. Service B contributes approximately **US$74 (₦100,000)**.

Both generate the same revenue, but Service A contributes twice as much toward covering operating expenses and profit.

If the business increasingly sells Service B, total revenue may grow while the average contribution per sale declines.

The same pattern can occur when certain clients require significantly more support, revisions, travel, or management time than others.

**What to examine:**

* Revenue and direct costs by service or product
* Contribution per sale
* Delivery hours and rework by project type
* Discounts and custom work by customer group
* The share of total sales generated by each offering

This does not automatically mean that lower-contribution services should be discontinued. They may support customer relationships, lead to other profitable work, or serve a strategic purpose.

First understand their full commercial role and the costs they create.

### 5. Is the Business Measuring the Right Financial Numbers?

A business cannot reliably diagnose declining profitability if its reporting only shows total sales and the bank balance.

Those figures are useful, but they do not explain the complete financial picture.

Cash in the bank may be affected by unpaid invoices, loan repayments, tax payments, equipment purchases, and other cash movements. Profit and cash flow are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Similarly, an increase in revenue may look encouraging until the business compares it with delivery costs and operating expenses over the same period.

**What to examine:**

At a minimum, review these measures consistently:

| Measure                   | What it helps you understand                                    |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Revenue                   | How much the business earns from sales                          |
| Gross profit              | What remains after the relevant direct costs                    |
| Gross profit margin       | The share of revenue retained after those direct costs          |
| Operating expenses        | The cost of running the business                                |
| Operating profit          | What remains from operations after the relevant operating costs |
| Operating profit margin   | The share of revenue retained as operating profit               |
| Cash flow and receivables | Whether cash is arriving in time to meet obligations            |

The exact measures and classifications should match the business's accounting practices. For reliable decisions, compare like with like: the same periods, consistent cost definitions, and figures that have been recorded accurately.

If the business cannot confidently explain how its profit margin changed, improving the quality of its financial reporting may need to come before a major pricing or growth decision.

## A Practical Example: More Revenue, Less Profit

Consider a hypothetical service business comparing two monthly periods.

| Measure                 |               Period 1 |               Period 2 |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------: | ---------------------: |
| Revenue                 | US$7,407 (₦10,000,000) | US$8,889 (₦12,000,000) |
| Direct delivery costs   |  US$3,704 (₦5,000,000) |  US$4,889 (₦6,600,000) |
| Gross profit            |  US$3,704 (₦5,000,000) |  US$4,000 (₦5,400,000) |

| Operating profit margin |                    20% |                    15% |

Revenue has increased by 20%, but operating profit has fallen by 10%.

The figures also reveal why.

Gross profit increased by approximately **US$296 (₦400,000)**, but operating expenses increased by approximately **US$444 (₦600,000)**. The additional gross profit did not cover the increase in operating expenses, so operating profit declined.

The next step is not to guess that marketing, pricing, or staffing must be the problem. It is to investigate the changes behind the numbers.

For example:

* Did the business incur higher costs to deliver its additional work?
* Did the mix of services change?
* Did it hire staff or take on new overhead?
* Did discounts reduce the contribution from sales?
* Were there one-off expenses that make the second period unusual?

The table identifies the financial change. It does not, by itself, establish the underlying cause.

That distinction is central to sound business diagnosis.

## How to Investigate Your Own Business

Use this sequence before making a major decision.

**Step 1: Compare equivalent periods.**
Review revenue, direct costs, gross profit, operating expenses, and operating profit for comparable periods. Account for seasonality and unusual one-off items where possible.

**Step 2: Identify where the change occurred.**
Determine whether the pressure appeared in direct costs, overhead, pricing, service mix, or another part of the financial picture.

**Step 3: Break down the relevant category.**
If direct costs increased, investigate the main cost components and the services generating them. If overhead increased, identify which expenses changed and why.

**Step 4: Connect the numbers to operational activity.**
Review delivery hours, project scope, staffing, discounts, rework, customer mix, or other relevant business records. Financial results tell you what changed; operational evidence can help explain why.

**Step 5: Choose a response that addresses the cause.**
Depending on what the evidence shows, the appropriate response may involve revising prices, improving delivery processes, reducing unnecessary costs, changing service scope, or adjusting the sales mix.

**Step 6: Measure the result.**
Establish a baseline and review the same measures after the change. This helps you determine whether the intervention improved profitability without creating new problems elsewhere.

## Should You Focus on More Sales or Better Profitability?

The answer depends on what the numbers reveal.

If your margins are healthy, delivery capacity is sufficient, and the business can serve additional customers profitably, generating more demand may be appropriate.

If each additional sale contributes too little toward overhead, or delivery costs are rising faster than revenue, increasing sales alone may intensify the problem.

If your financial records are incomplete, the first priority may be improving measurement before deciding what to change.

The important question is not simply, *How can we generate more revenue?*

It is:

**What is preventing the business from turning its revenue into the level of profit it needs?**

That question connects financial performance to the wider business system. Pricing, sales, delivery, staffing, customer mix, and operating processes can influence one another. The right intervention depends on which constraint is actually affecting the business.

When revenue grows but profit does not, there is rarely enough information in the headline sales figure to justify a confident decision.

The business needs to establish where the financial pressure is occurring, investigate the causes, and determine which changes are likely to address it.

**How to Price Your Services Without Guessing or Undercutting Yourself**

Your prices need to make sense for both your business and your customers. Learn how to assess delivery costs, profit requirements, market alternatives, and customer value before setting or changing your service prices.

Article 5 full draft:
# How to Price Your Services Without Guessing or Undercutting Yourself

How much should you charge for your services?

For many business owners, the answer comes from a mixture of experience, competitor research, customer expectations, and intuition.

You might check what other businesses charge, estimate how long the work will take, and choose a price that feels reasonable. If a prospect objects, you may consider reducing the price to win the business.

These approaches can provide useful information, but they become risky when they replace a clear pricing process.

A price that attracts customers may not cover the cost of delivering the work. A price that protects your margin may be difficult to justify if customers cannot understand the value. And a price copied from a competitor may be unsuitable for a business with different costs, capabilities, or customers.

**The goal is not simply to charge more. It is to set a price that customers can understand, the market can support, and the business can sustain.**

Here is how to approach that decision.

*Currency note: Naira equivalents throughout this article use an illustrative reference rate of US$1 = ₦1,350, dated 14 October 2026, as specified for these examples. Exchange rates vary by source, transaction type, and date. These conversions are illustrative, not guaranteed transaction rates. Verify the applicable rate before using the figures for financial decisions.*

## 1. Stop Treating Your Competitors' Prices as Your Pricing Strategy

Competitor research can help you understand the alternatives available to your customers. It cannot tell you exactly what your business should charge.

Consider two businesses offering website development.

One uses a standardized process, has a clearly defined scope, and can complete most projects efficiently. The other provides extensive consultation, custom integrations, copywriting, and ongoing support.

Even if both businesses describe their offer as website development, they may be selling substantially different services.

Their costs, delivery processes, risks, and customer outcomes may differ.

Matching the lower price simply because another provider charges it could leave one business with insufficient margin. Charging significantly more without explaining the difference could make the offer difficult to evaluate.

Instead, investigate three things:

* **The offer:** What exactly is included, and what is excluded?
* **The economics:** What does it cost your business to deliver the work?
* **The buyer's decision:** What alternatives is the customer considering, and what matters when choosing between them?

Compare services that are genuinely comparable. A headline price alone rarely reveals the full scope, delivery quality, support, or commercial terms behind an offer.

Competitor research should help you understand the market, not dictate your price.

## 2. Calculate What It Actually Costs to Deliver Your Service

Before deciding what to charge, establish the cost of fulfilling the work.

For a service business, relevant costs may include:

* Staff time spent delivering the service
* Payments to freelancers or subcontractors
* Materials, licences, or third-party tools used for a project
* Travel and other delivery expenses
* Payment processing fees
* Rework and additional effort caused by the scope of the engagement

The appropriate classification depends on the business. Some costs change with each project, while others remain relatively stable over a period.

You also need to account for the wider costs of operating the business, such as administration, rent, software subscriptions, insurance, and management time.

Suppose a consultant estimates that a project requires 20 hours of work. If the actual delivery repeatedly takes 32 hours, the original estimate understates the resources required.

The quoted price may appear profitable on paper while the real return is much lower.

**What to investigate:**

1. How much time does the service actually require?
2. Which direct costs are incurred for each engagement?
3. What additional work regularly falls outside the original scope?
4. Which operating expenses must the business cover?
5. How much capacity does the service consume?

Use actual delivery records where possible, rather than relying exclusively on estimates.

The US Small Business Administration's break-even guidance explains why businesses need to account for fixed costs, variable costs, and the contribution generated by each sale when evaluating pricing decisions. The precise calculations should be adapted to the business's actual cost structure.

## 3. Understand the Difference Between Markup and Margin

This distinction matters because confusing the two can result in prices that are lower than intended.

Imagine that delivering a service costs **US$100 (₦135,000)**.

If you add a 30% markup, the calculation is:

US$100 × 1.30 = **US$130 (₦175,500)**.

The resulting contribution before other business expenses is **US$30 (₦40,500)**.

But that contribution represents approximately 23.1% of the selling price, not 30%.

The reason is that markup is calculated relative to cost, while margin is calculated relative to selling price.

If you want a 30% gross margin on a service with a direct delivery cost of US$100 (₦135,000), the calculation is different:

**Price = Direct cost ÷ (1 − Target gross margin)**

Therefore:

US$100 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = approximately **US$142.86 (₦192,861)**.

At that price, the difference between the selling price and the direct cost is approximately US$42.86 (₦57,861), which is 30% of the selling price.

These figures are illustrative. They assume the stated direct cost is complete and do not mean that a 30% margin is appropriate for every business.

More importantly, gross margin is not the same as net profit margin. The business still needs to cover relevant operating expenses and other applicable costs.

Use the margin you actually need as a planning input, not a percentage copied from another company.

## 4. Consider What the Customer Is Really Buying

A service is rarely valuable to a customer simply because it requires a certain number of hours to deliver.

Customers may be paying for expertise, convenience, reduced risk, better execution, saved time, or an outcome they cannot easily achieve themselves.

Research published in the *Journal of Product & Brand Management* examined willingness to pay for professional services using two consumer surveys, with samples of 488 and 479 respondents. The research found that behavioural factors, including customers' confidence in their own ability and their perceptions of the service provider's outcome, were relevant to willingness to pay.

The study does not establish what every customer will pay today. It does, however, support an important principle: **the customer's assessment of the service matters alongside its financial price.**

Consider a business that offers CRM implementation.

One provider may describe the offer as setting up a CRM platform. Another may explain how the work will help the client organise inquiries, assign follow-up responsibility, and track opportunities that might otherwise be overlooked.

The second description connects the work to a business problem. That can help the buyer understand why the service may be useful.

But better positioning does not automatically justify any price. The provider still needs to demonstrate credible capabilities, define the scope, and explain what the customer can reasonably expect.

To assess customer value, investigate:

* What problem is the customer trying to solve?
* What happens if the problem remains unresolved?
* What alternatives can the customer realistically choose?
* What does the customer expect the service to improve?
* How much uncertainty or risk remains after considering your offer?
* What evidence supports the outcome you describe?

Do not invent financial benefits to justify a higher price. If you cannot demonstrate a specific financial return, explain the service's scope, purpose, and realistic benefits without promising an outcome you cannot substantiate.

## 5. Test Your Price Against the Market

A price can cover your costs and still fail commercially if customers do not see enough value in the offer or if suitable alternatives are more attractive.

This is why pricing requires market evidence as well as financial calculations.

Start with conversations with qualified prospects and existing customers. Ask what they are trying to achieve, which alternatives they have considered, what matters most in their decision, and what concerns they have about your offer.

Pay attention to actual behaviour, not just compliments or hypothetical answers.

Useful evidence includes:

* Which offers receive serious consideration?
* How often do qualified prospects object to the price?
* What reasons do prospects give for declining?
* Which alternatives do they compare with your offer?
* Do customers understand the difference between your service and lower-priced alternatives?
* How do acceptance rates differ across relevant customer groups or offers?

A lost sale does not automatically mean the price is too high. The prospect may not understand the offer, may not trust the provider, may have a different priority, or may not be an appropriate customer.

Likewise, a high acceptance rate does not prove that the price is optimal. You may be charging less than customers would reasonably accept, or attracting work that is not profitable to deliver.

Interpret pricing evidence alongside the full sales process and the economics of the service.

## 6. Be Careful with Discounts

Discounting can help win business, but it should be treated as a commercial decision rather than an automatic response to resistance.

Suppose a service sells for **US$200 (₦270,000)** and has a direct delivery cost of **US$120 (₦162,000)**.

Before other expenses, the contribution from the sale is:

US$200 − US$120 = **US$80 (₦108,000)**.

Now suppose the business reduces the price by 10%, to **US$180 (₦243,000)**, while the delivery cost remains US$120 (₦162,000).

The contribution becomes:

US$180 − US$120 = **US$60 (₦81,000)**.

The price has fallen by 10%, but the contribution per sale has fallen by 25%.

To generate the same total contribution as before, the business would need to complete more sales.

At the original price, five sales generate **US$400 (₦540,000)** in contribution.

At the discounted price, generating that same contribution requires approximately 6.67 sales. In practical terms, the business would need seven sales to exceed the original contribution from five sales.

This comparison assumes the delivery cost remains constant per sale and excludes other operating expenses. It is an illustration, not a forecast.

The commercial implication is straightforward: a discount may require additional sales and delivery capacity just to recover the contribution given up.

Before discounting, ask:

* Is the prospect objecting to the price or to the value they perceive?
* Can the scope be adjusted to fit the available budget?
* Would a different package better match the customer's needs?
* Is the discount tied to a legitimate commercial condition?
* Will the sale still contribute enough to cover the business's costs?
* Can the business deliver the additional volume without reducing quality?

Sometimes the right response is to revise the scope rather than reduce the price for the same amount of work.

If the requested price makes the engagement commercially unviable, declining the work may be more sensible than accepting it at a loss.

## 7. Define the Scope Before You Quote

Unclear scope can undermine even a well-calculated price.

A business may quote for a specific deliverable, only to discover that the customer expects additional meetings, revisions, integrations, reporting, or ongoing support.

If these expectations are not addressed, the provider may absorb the extra work or face a difficult conversation about additional charges.

Before issuing a quote, clarify:

* The work to be completed
* The deliverables the customer will receive
* The information or access the customer must provide
* The expected timeline and dependencies
* The number of revisions or changes included
* The support provided after delivery
* The conditions that require additional fees

This is especially important for services where the work can expand as new requirements emerge.

Clear scope protects both parties. The customer knows what is being purchased, and the provider has a more reliable basis for estimating costs and delivery capacity.

A transparent proposal also makes price comparisons more meaningful because customers can evaluate what each provider is actually offering.

## 8. A Practical Framework for Setting Your Price

Use this sequence when pricing a new service or reviewing an existing one.

**Step 1: Define the offer.**

Specify the problem the service addresses, the work involved, the deliverables, and the boundaries of the engagement.

**Step 2: Estimate the full delivery cost.**

Include realistic labour, third-party expenses, materials, and other costs associated with fulfilling the work. Use historical delivery records where available.

**Step 3: Establish the financial requirement.**

Determine the contribution the service needs to generate and how it supports the business's wider operating costs and profit objectives.

**Step 4: Review comparable market alternatives.**

Compare the scope, expertise, delivery model, support, and commercial terms of relevant alternatives. Avoid treating a competitor's advertised price as proof of what your business should charge.

**Step 5: Assess customer value and buying behaviour.**

Investigate the customer's problem, desired outcome, alternatives, concerns, and reasons for choosing or rejecting the offer.

**Step 6: Set a proposed price and test the assumptions.**

Check whether the price covers the relevant costs, contributes toward the financial objective, and is defensible given the offer and market evidence.

**Step 7: Track what happens after the price is introduced.**

Record quotes, acceptance rates, reasons for lost opportunities, discounts, actual delivery hours, and the contribution generated by completed work.

Review these results over time. A price should not be judged only by whether it wins a sale; it should also be judged by the commercial quality of the work it attracts.

## When Should You Raise Your Prices?

A price review may be appropriate when delivery costs increase, the scope of work expands, the business's capabilities change, or market evidence suggests the current offer and price no longer fit.

However, a price increase is not always the right answer.

If the service is unprofitable because it takes too long to deliver, improving the process may be necessary. If prospects consistently misunderstand the offer, clearer positioning may help. If customers require a smaller engagement, a more appropriate package may be the answer.

If the business cannot explain its costs or contribution, better measurement should come before a major pricing decision.

The question is not simply whether your prices are high enough.

It is whether the offer, price, delivery model, and customer expectations work together commercially.

## Price for a Business You Can Sustain

Pricing is not a one-time guess. It is a decision that should be informed by delivery costs, financial objectives, customer value, market alternatives, and actual buying behaviour.

When those factors are considered together, a business is better equipped to avoid unnecessary discounting, recognise unprofitable work, and make deliberate decisions about which services and customers to pursue.

# More Sales, Less Profit: The Hidden Economics of Service Delivery

Winning more customers should be good news for any service business. More projects, more bookings, and more contracts should create opportunities to increase revenue and grow the company.

But there is a problem business owners can overlook: **the more work the business wins, the more it may cost to deliver that work.**

A project that looked profitable when it was quoted may require more staff hours than expected. A client may request repeated revisions. Materials may cost more than anticipated. A team may spend valuable time correcting mistakes, chasing approvals, or completing work that was never clearly included in the original agreement.

Individually, these problems can seem manageable. Across multiple projects, however, they can consume the contribution that sales were supposed to generate.

The result is a business that looks busier but does not necessarily become more profitable.

The answer is not automatically to reduce costs, increase prices, or turn away customers. It is to understand the economics of delivering the work and identify where the original plan differs from reality.

## More Revenue Does Not Automatically Mean Better Delivery Economics

Revenue tells you how much the business earns from selling its services. It does not tell you how much remains after the work has been delivered.

Consider a service business that wins more projects this year than it did last year. Revenue rises, but the team also spends more time on each project, hires additional contractors, handles more revisions, and works longer hours to meet deadlines.

If the extra revenue is consumed by the additional delivery costs, the business may have little improvement in profit despite its higher sales.

This is why owners should examine profitability at more than the company-wide level. Where possible, they should also understand the economics of individual projects, service lines, or client engagements.

The U.S. Small Business Administration's guidance on break-even analysis distinguishes the selling price of a service from its variable costs and explains how the difference contributes toward covering fixed expenses. The same principle is useful when reviewing service delivery: the amount invoiced is not the same as the amount available to cover overhead and generate profit. See the [U.S. Small Business Administration's break-even guidance](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/plan-your-business/).

For a service business, the practical question is:

**After accounting for the resources required to deliver this work, how much does each project contribute toward the cost of running the business and generating profit?**

Answering that question requires more than looking at sales reports.

## Five Hidden Costs That Can Weaken Service Profitability

### 1. Labour and time that exceed the original estimate

Time is one of the most important resources in a service business. Yet owners do not always measure how much time a project actually consumes.

A consultant may quote for a defined engagement but spend additional hours preparing reports and answering unplanned questions. A web development team may estimate a project at 40 hours but spend 60 hours completing it. A contractor may underestimate the labour required to complete an installation.

The client may receive the agreed deliverable, and the business may collect the agreed fee. But if delivery takes substantially longer than expected, the economics of the project have changed.

This matters even when employees receive fixed salaries. Their time still has a cost, and hours consumed by one project cannot always be used to deliver another.

**What to investigate:**

* Estimated hours versus actual hours per project.
* Labour cost per project.
* Which tasks regularly take longer than planned.
* Whether the original estimate omitted important work.
* Whether delays or interruptions are reducing productive capacity.

Do not assume every overrun means the team is inefficient. The estimate may have been unrealistic, the client requirements may have changed, or the delivery process may be poorly designed.

The objective is to find the reason for the difference.

### 2. Rework, corrections, and repeated revisions

Rework occurs when a business must repeat or correct work that has already been completed or attempted.

It can result from unclear requirements, design errors, inadequate quality checks, incorrect information, or changes made after work has started.

Research on selected commercial building projects in Abuja, Nigeria, found a significant relationship between rework costs and project cost and time performance. The study reported that rework had an average impact of 3.53% on initial project cost across the projects examined. This is evidence from a specific construction sample, not a universal estimate for service businesses. Nevertheless, it illustrates how correcting work can affect both delivery costs and schedules. [Read the study in the *International Journal of Built Environment and Sustainability*.](https://doi.org/10.11113/ijbes.v5.n1.246)

The same underlying issue can appear in other service settings, although the causes and costs will differ.

An agency may redo a campaign because the original brief was incomplete. A consultant may revise a report after discovering that the client supplied inaccurate data. A professional services firm may spend additional time correcting errors that should have been caught during review.

Some revisions are a normal part of good service. The problem is not revision itself. The problem is recurring, avoidable rework that consumes time and resources without creating additional revenue.

**What to investigate:**

* How much time is spent correcting completed work.
* The most common causes of revisions or errors.
* Whether requirements were documented and approved.
* Whether quality checks happen early enough.
* Whether repeated problems affect particular services or project types.

If the same type of correction keeps appearing, the business may need to improve its process rather than simply ask staff to work faster.

### 3. Scope creep and unplanned work

Scope creep occurs when the work required to complete an engagement expands beyond what was originally agreed, often without a corresponding increase in fees.

It may begin with a request that sounds harmless:

* “Could you make one more change?”
* “Can you include this additional feature?”
* “Could you handle this related task while you're at it?”
* “Can we have another meeting to go through everything again?”

The business agrees because it wants to protect the client relationship. One request does not seem significant enough to challenge.

But repeated additions can gradually change the amount of work required to complete the engagement.

A 2025 survey of managed service providers, commissioned by project-management software company Moovila and conducted by The Channel Company, identified scope creep as a major project-management challenge. The findings are relevant to that sector, but they should not be interpreted as a measured rate for all service businesses. [Read the survey information from Moovila.](https://www.moovilla.com/pr-2025-research-report)

The financial issue is straightforward: **when the work expands but the agreed fee does not, the cost of delivering the original sale can increase without additional revenue.**

**What to investigate:**

* Whether project scope is documented before work begins.
* How additional requests are assessed and approved.
* Whether the contract defines revision limits and exclusions.
* How frequently work is completed without additional payment.
* Whether the team can distinguish necessary corrections from new client requests.

A scope-control process does not have to be complicated. The business needs a clear way to identify additional work, explain its implications, and agree on the next step with the client.

Depending on the agreement, that may mean adjusting the scope, extending the timeline, or quoting for additional work.

### 4. Materials, subcontractors, and other direct delivery costs

Labour is not the only cost that can increase as a business grows.

A contractor may pay more for materials than originally estimated. A consultant may need specialist support for a project. An installation business may incur extra transport costs. A marketing agency may use external production services that were not fully accounted for in the original quote.

If the selling price stays the same while these costs increase, the contribution from the project falls.

This is why businesses should compare the costs estimated before work begins with the actual costs recorded after delivery.

**What to investigate:**

* Estimated versus actual materials and subcontractor costs.
* Additional transport, equipment, or production expenses.
* Supplier price changes between quotation and delivery.
* Costs that were omitted from the original estimate.
* Whether particular services or project types consistently exceed budget.

Do not focus only on large expenses. Small cost overruns repeated across many projects can become commercially significant.

### 5. Capacity lost to delays and inefficient processes

Not every delivery cost appears as a separate payment.

Time spent waiting for approvals, chasing information, correcting avoidable mistakes, or coordinating unclear responsibilities can reduce the time available for other work.

For example, a team may have enough technical capacity to deliver ten projects in a month under normal conditions. But if projects repeatedly stall because clients have not supplied information, managers have to resolve preventable problems, or tasks are poorly coordinated, the team may complete fewer projects than expected.

The business may then struggle to accept new work without extending deadlines or increasing staffing costs.

A 2026 professional-services benchmark published by Deltek, drawing on the SPI Research benchmark, reported that billable utilisation across the firms surveyed fell to 66.4% in 2025. The report also recorded an average project margin of 37.7% and EBITDA of 9.9% for that benchmark population. These are sector-specific benchmark figures, not targets that every service business should adopt. They demonstrate why professional-services firms monitor utilisation, project economics, and overall profitability separately. [Read Deltek's summary of the 2026 professional-services benchmarks.](https://www.deltek.com/resources/articles/2026-professional-services-benchmarks/)

For your own business, the useful question is not whether you match an industry benchmark. It is whether your available capacity is being used in a way that supports profitable delivery.

**What to investigate:**

* How much time is spent on planned versus unplanned work.
* How often projects wait for information or approvals.
* Whether the same people are repeatedly overloaded.
* Which bottlenecks delay completion.
* Whether the business has enough capacity to accept additional work without harming quality or deadlines.

Capacity problems may require better planning, clearer responsibilities, improved workflows, or changes to the service model. They do not automatically mean the business needs more staff or new software.

Now imagine that the business becomes busier and completes more projects like Project B. Sales increase, but the business may be using significantly more resources to generate each additional dollar of revenue.

Avoid measuring everything at once. A small set of consistently recorded metrics is more useful than a sophisticated dashboard filled with incomplete data.

## How to Investigate Delivery Costs in Practice

### Step 1: Review recently completed work

Choose a sample of recent projects or client engagements. Include both work that performed well and work that exceeded its expected cost or timeline.

Record the fee, estimated delivery time, actual delivery time, direct costs, major changes in scope, and any significant rework.

Start with information you can reasonably verify. Do not treat guesses as actual costs simply because the figures are convenient.

### Step 2: Compare the plan with what happened

Look for differences between the original estimate and actual delivery.

Did the work take longer than expected? Were additional materials required? Did the client request more work? Did the team repeat tasks because the requirements were unclear?

The goal is to identify patterns, not to blame the person who recorded the estimate or completed the work.

### Step 3: Identify the recurring cause

A single project overrun may be unusual. The same problem across several projects is a stronger reason to investigate the process.

For example, repeated overruns may point to:

* Estimates that consistently omit necessary tasks.
* Unclear project scope.
* A handover process that creates avoidable errors.
* Late client approvals.
* A service that requires more custom work than the business originally assumed.

Different causes require different responses. Better estimation will not solve a poorly defined scope, and a new project-management tool will not automatically correct an unrealistic delivery promise.

### Step 4: Choose a targeted correction

Once you have identified a credible cause, make a specific change that addresses it.

That might involve defining the scope more clearly, introducing an approval checkpoint, improving the handover between sales and delivery, revising the project estimate, documenting recurring tasks, or changing how additional work is quoted.

Avoid changing multiple parts of the process simultaneously if you cannot track the effect. A focused test makes it easier to understand whether the intervention is helping.

### Step 5: Review the results

Compare the next set of similar projects with the earlier baseline.

Did actual delivery hours move closer to estimates? Did rework decline? Did contribution improve? Were projects completed on time more consistently?

If the results do not improve, revisit the diagnosis. The original explanation may have been incomplete, or the proposed change may not have addressed the underlying cause.

## The Answer Is Not Always to Cut Costs

When delivery costs rise, cutting expenditure may appear to be the fastest response. But indiscriminate cost reduction can create new problems.

Reducing quality checks may increase rework. Overloading staff may delay projects. Removing necessary customer communication may weaken the client experience. Hiring less experienced people without adequate support may increase supervision and correction costs.

Instead, determine whether the underlying issue concerns the way work is estimated, sold, scoped, scheduled, delivered, or reviewed.

The appropriate intervention depends on the cause.

* If the scope keeps expanding, strengthen scope definition and change control.
* If projects regularly exceed their estimated hours, investigate the assumptions behind the estimates.
* If errors repeatedly require corrections, examine requirements, training, handovers, and quality checks.
* If work stalls while waiting for information or approval, review responsibilities and dependencies.
* If a service consistently consumes more resources than its fee can support, assess its pricing, scope, delivery model, or commercial viability.

Some businesses will need process improvements. Others may need to redesign a service, change the way they price projects, improve capacity planning, or stop accepting work that does not fit their operating model.

The point is not to make every project cheaper at any cost. It is to create a delivery system that can support growth without allowing avoidable complexity to consume the value of each sale.

Before assuming the business needs more customers, more staff, or another tool, examine what happens after a sale is made.

Compare estimates with actual costs. Identify where work expands or gets repeated. Measure the contribution generated by different projects. Then investigate the causes before deciding what needs to change.

# How to Diagnose a Weak Sales Pipeline Before Buying More Leads

When sales are inconsistent, the natural response is often to look for more prospects.

A business might increase its advertising budget, purchase a prospect list, hire another salesperson, or launch a new marketing campaign. The thinking is straightforward: if more opportunities enter the pipeline, more sales should follow.

But that assumption can be expensive.

If a business is attracting the wrong prospects, responding inconsistently, failing to qualify opportunities, or allowing potential deals to stall, generating more leads may simply increase the number of opportunities it fails to convert.

The problem may not be the amount of demand entering the pipeline. It may be what happens after prospects enter it.

Before spending more to generate leads, examine how your sales pipeline actually performs. The objective is to determine whether you have a volume problem, a conversion problem, a follow-up problem, or a combination of several issues.

**More leads can create more opportunities, but only a functioning sales process can turn those opportunities into customers.**

## What Is a Sales Pipeline, and Why Does It Matter?

A sales pipeline is a structured view of potential business moving through the stages between initial contact and a completed sale.

The stages differ by business, but a service business might track the following:

1. **New prospect:** A potential customer has been identified or has made contact.
2. **Contact established:** The business has engaged with the prospect.
3. **Qualified opportunity:** There is a credible fit between the prospect's needs and the service offered.
4. **Proposal or quotation:** The business has presented a proposed solution and its terms.
5. **Decision pending:** The prospect is evaluating the proposal, seeking approval, or discussing next steps.
6. **Won or lost:** The opportunity has resulted in a sale or has been closed without one.

Not every business needs these exact stages. A dental clinic, consulting practice, commercial contractor, and B2B service provider may have different customer journeys.

What matters is that the stages reflect real progress in the buying process rather than merely recording that someone has been contacted.

For example, sending a quotation does not mean a prospect is close to buying. A prospect who has confirmed a need, agreed on the scope, and scheduled a decision meeting may represent a more advanced opportunity than someone who simply requested a price.

A useful pipeline helps the business distinguish activity from progress.

It also makes it easier to see where opportunities accumulate, where they stop moving, and which stages need investigation.

## First, Determine Whether You Actually Have a Lead-Volume Problem

Before diagnosing conversion, establish whether enough suitable prospects are entering the pipeline in the first place.

A business can have a healthy conversion process and still struggle to generate sufficient sales if too few qualified opportunities enter it.

The reverse is also possible: the business may attract many inquiries but convert very few into paying customers.

These situations require different responses.

Consider three possible scenarios:

| What you observe                                 | What it may indicate                                  | What to investigate                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Few qualified opportunities enter the pipeline   | A possible demand or targeting problem                | Marketing reach, audience fit, referral sources, and inquiry volume                  |
| Many prospects enter, but few qualify            | A possible targeting, offer, or qualification problem | Prospect needs, budget, fit, expectations, and acquisition channels                  |
| Many prospects qualify, but few become customers | A possible sales-process problem                      | Follow-up, proposal quality, objections, decision timelines, and conversion by stage |

These are starting hypotheses, not conclusions. Low conversion could have several causes, and low volume might be a consequence of limited market demand rather than weak marketing execution.

The first task is to identify what the available data supports.

### What the research tells us

Sales productivity is one reason to examine the process before automatically adding more activity.

In its February 2026 sales research summary, Salesforce reported that the average seller spends about 40% of their time actually selling. This is a reported finding from Salesforce's research, not a universal figure for every sales team or small service business. It nevertheless highlights a practical issue: sales capacity can be consumed by administration and other activities rather than direct customer engagement.

For a small business owner who handles sales alongside delivery and operations, the constraint may be even more specific. There may not be enough time to respond promptly, prepare proposals, follow up, and move existing opportunities forward.

That does not automatically mean the answer is automation or another employee. First, determine where time is being spent and which activities are preventing opportunities from progressing.

Source: [Salesforce's sales statistics and research](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stat-library/all-stats/).

## Five Areas to Examine Before Buying More Leads

### 1. Are You Attracting the Right Prospects?

A prospect is not automatically a good sales opportunity simply because they have made contact.

Some prospects may need a service the business does not provide. Others may have unrealistic expectations, insufficient budget, no immediate need, or no authority to make a purchasing decision.

If these prospects enter the pipeline in large numbers, the business may spend considerable time handling inquiries that were unlikely to become customers.

The problem could originate in marketing messages that attract the wrong audience, an offer that is too broad, or a qualification process that does not identify fit early enough.

**What to investigate:**

* Which channels generate the most qualified opportunities?
* Which channels generate high inquiry volume but few sales?
* What needs or problems do successful customers share?
* How many inquiries fall outside the service's intended scope?
* Are prospects clear about what the business offers and who it serves?

Compare outcomes by acquisition source where possible. A channel that generates fewer inquiries may still be more commercially valuable if those inquiries are a better fit and progress into sales more consistently.

Do not judge a channel only by the number of leads it produces. Examine the quality and commercial outcome of the opportunities it generates.

### 2. Are You Qualifying Opportunities Properly?

Qualification is the process of determining whether a prospect is a credible potential customer for a particular service.

It does not mean judging people prematurely or rejecting anyone who does not fit a rigid checklist. It means gathering enough information to understand the prospect's needs and whether the business can reasonably help.

Depending on the service, qualification may involve questions such as:

* What problem is the prospect trying to solve?
* How important is solving it?
* Does the requested service fit the business's capabilities?
* Is there a realistic budget or purchasing process?
* Who is involved in making the decision?
* What timeline does the prospect have in mind?

Without qualification, a pipeline can appear full while containing many opportunities that are unlikely to progress.

A consultant might spend hours preparing proposals for businesses that need a completely different service. A contractor might quote projects without confirming site conditions. A B2B provider might spend weeks pursuing an opportunity with no agreed purchasing timeline.

The cost is not limited to lost sales. It includes the time spent on opportunities that were never commercially viable.

**What to investigate:**

Track how many prospects advance from initial contact to qualified opportunity, and record the main reasons others do not.

If qualification rates are consistently low, investigate the targeting, offer, and initial sales conversation before increasing lead volume.

### 3. Are Opportunities Moving Through the Pipeline?

A pipeline can contain many opportunities and still produce disappointing sales if those opportunities remain in the same stages for too long.

A proposal may sit unanswered. A prospect may express interest without agreeing to a next step. A quotation may be sent, but nobody follows up to understand whether the customer has questions or concerns.

These situations can make the pipeline look more promising than it really is.

To diagnose the problem, examine both conversion rates and the time opportunities spend in each stage.

For example, if a large number of opportunities reach the proposal stage but relatively few become sales, the business should investigate what happens after proposals are sent.

Possible explanations include:

* The proposal does not address the prospect's main concern.
* The scope or price does not match the prospect's expectations.
* The prospect is comparing other providers.
* The decision process involves people the business has not engaged.
* The next step is unclear.
* The opportunity was never as advanced as the pipeline suggested.

The stage itself does not reveal the cause. It identifies where to investigate.

**What to investigate:**

* How many opportunities advance from each stage?
* How long do they typically remain there?
* How many have no recorded next step?
* How many have had their expected closing date repeatedly postponed?
* What reasons do prospects give when they decide not to proceed?

A simple pipeline review can reveal whether the business is creating genuine momentum or merely accumulating open opportunities.

### 4. Is Follow-Up Consistent and Useful?

A prospect who does not respond immediately is not necessarily uninterested.

They may be busy, comparing alternatives, waiting for approval, discussing the decision with colleagues, or unsure whether the proposed service is right for them.

Without a follow-up process, the business may abandon opportunities before understanding what is preventing a decision.

Follow-up should not mean repeatedly sending generic messages asking whether the prospect is ready to buy. It should help the prospect make progress.

Depending on the situation, that may involve answering a question, clarifying the proposal, providing relevant information, discussing a concern, or agreeing on when to reconnect.

**What to investigate:**

* Is every active opportunity assigned to someone responsible for it?
* Are next steps and follow-up dates recorded?
* Are inquiries receiving a response within a reasonable period?
* Are follow-ups relevant to the prospect's needs and previous conversations?
* Are opportunities being marked as lost simply because they stopped responding?
* Does the business record the outcome of follow-up attempts?

The goal is to create a repeatable process, not to contact every prospect indefinitely.

When a prospect is no longer interested, has chosen another provider, or is not a suitable fit, record that outcome. Accurate records are more valuable than a pipeline full of opportunities that are unlikely to close.

### 5. Are You Learning Why Sales Are Won or Lost?

If the business records only the final outcome, it may miss important information about its sales process.

For example, losing a deal because the service was outside the prospect's budget is different from losing it because the business did not respond, failed to explain the value, or proposed a solution that did not fit the need.

These outcomes call for different responses.

Price objections may require better qualification, clearer value communication, a different scope, or a review of the target market. Repeated losses caused by delayed responses may point to a follow-up or capacity problem. Lost opportunities due to poor fit may indicate that the marketing message is attracting the wrong audience.

Record reasons for lost opportunities consistently, but avoid treating a prospect's stated reason as definitive proof of the underlying cause. A prospect may say the price was too high when the actual concern was uncertainty about value.

Look for patterns across multiple opportunities rather than making major changes based on one lost sale.

**What to investigate:**

* What are the most common recorded reasons for lost opportunities?
* Do losses vary by service, customer type, or acquisition channel?
* Are opportunities lost at a particular pipeline stage?
* Are similar objections appearing repeatedly?
* What do successful sales have in common?

This information helps the business improve its sales process based on observed outcomes rather than assumptions.

Consider a service business that records 100 new inquiries during a particular period.

That number alone does not tell the owner what is wrong. But the stage-level figures provide a starting point.

Half the inquiries qualify. Of the qualified opportunities, 60% reach the proposal stage. Of those receiving proposals, 30% become paying customers.

The business should now investigate the meaning behind those numbers.

Are half the inquiries unqualified because marketing attracts the wrong audience? Are some prospects being disqualified appropriately because the service is not right for them? Are proposals being sent to people who have not confirmed their needs or buying intentions? Are prospects declining after seeing the price? Are follow-ups being missed?

The data identifies where questions should be asked. It does not answer those questions by itself.

The inquiry-to-customer conversion rate rises from 9% to 12%.

That is an increase of 3 percentage points, or a 33.3% relative improvement.

If the business can generate more customers from the same inquiry volume, it may be able to improve sales without immediately increasing lead-generation expenditure.

However, it should also examine customer value, delivery capacity, margins, and the cost of implementing the improvement. More customers are commercially useful only when the business can serve them appropriately and profitably.

At 9 customers, that is US$9,000 (₦12,150,000) in revenue.

## How to Conduct a Practical Sales Pipeline Review

You do not need a complex CRM or a sophisticated analytics platform to start. A spreadsheet may be enough for a small business with a manageable number of opportunities.

Begin with these steps.

### Step 1: Define your pipeline stages

Use stages that reflect meaningful progress in your actual sales process.

Make sure the team understands what must happen before an opportunity advances. Sending a proposal, for example, should be recorded as a stage change only when the proposal has genuinely been sent.

### Step 2: Record every active opportunity consistently

For each opportunity, record the prospect, service required, acquisition source, current stage, date of entry, person responsible, next action, expected decision date, and final outcome.

Do not collect information that nobody will use. Prioritise the data needed to understand movement, conversion, and commercial value.

### Step 3: Calculate conversion between stages

For each stage, compare the number of opportunities that entered it with the number that progressed to the next stage.

Make sure the figures refer to a comparable group of opportunities. Comparing opportunities that entered a stage last week with sales that closed this week can be misleading when the sales cycle is longer.

Where possible, track cohorts of opportunities over time and allow for the length of the buying process.

### Step 4: Examine stalled opportunities

Identify opportunities that have remained in the same stage longer than is normal for your business.

Check whether they have a clear next step, whether the prospect is still engaged, and whether the expected decision date is realistic.

Update or close opportunities that are no longer active. This produces a more useful view of the pipeline than retaining every historical inquiry as a potential sale.

### Step 5: Record outcomes and investigate patterns

Record why opportunities are won, lost, disqualified, or deferred.

Review the results by acquisition channel, service, customer type, and pipeline stage where the volume of data is sufficient to support a meaningful comparison.

Be cautious with small samples. A difference based on a handful of opportunities may reflect chance or unusual circumstances rather than a persistent problem.

### Step 6: Test one targeted improvement

Choose a change that addresses a credible problem identified by the review.

For example, if qualified opportunities regularly stall after proposals are sent, test a clearer proposal structure and a defined follow-up process. If a particular acquisition channel generates many unsuitable inquiries, review its targeting and messaging.

Measure the result against the original baseline before deciding whether to keep, change, or abandon the intervention.

Before increasing spend, ask:

* Are the existing inquiries reasonably well matched to the service?
* Can the business respond and follow up consistently?
* Does the business understand its conversion rates by stage?
* Is the current pipeline producing enough sales to support its targets?
* Can the business deliver additional work without compromising quality or profitability?
* Can the business measure the cost and commercial outcome of the additional leads?

If the answers reveal that the sales process is weak, address the relevant constraint before treating higher lead volume as the primary solution.

If the pipeline is functioning well but lacks sufficient qualified opportunities, increasing marketing activity may be justified. The business should still set a budget, define the outcome it expects, and measure the results.

There is no universal conversion rate that every service business must achieve. Sales cycles, service prices, customer types, buying decisions, and acquisition channels differ.

The useful benchmark is your own performance over time, interpreted in the context of your business model and the quality of the opportunities entering the pipeline.

# CRM vs Spreadsheet: When Does a Business Actually Need a CRM?

When a business starts losing track of customer inquiries, missing follow-ups, or struggling to understand its sales pipeline, one solution often comes to mind: get a customer relationship management system, commonly called a CRM.

The recommendation sounds reasonable. A CRM can organise customer records, track sales opportunities, assign follow-up tasks, and automate parts of the sales process.

But buying software does not automatically fix a broken process.

A business can invest in a CRM and still lose opportunities if nobody updates the records, follow-up responsibilities remain unclear, or the team does not use the system consistently. Equally, a small business with a straightforward sales process may manage its customers perfectly well with a spreadsheet.

The real question is not whether a CRM is more advanced than a spreadsheet.

**It is whether your current system can reliably support the way your business manages prospects, customers, and sales opportunities.**

Before switching tools, examine what is going wrong, how often it happens, and whether a CRM would solve the actual problem.

## What Is the Difference Between a Spreadsheet and a CRM?

A spreadsheet is a flexible way to organise information in rows and columns. A business can use it to record customer names, contact details, inquiry sources, sales stages, expected values, and follow-up dates.

For a business with a small number of opportunities managed by one person, this may be all that is required.

A CRM is designed specifically to manage customer relationships and related activities. Depending on the product and configuration, it may centralise contact records, retain interaction histories, track sales stages, assign ownership, create reminders, automate workflows, and report on pipeline performance.

The difference is not simply the amount of data either tool can store. Spreadsheet software can handle large datasets. Microsoft's documentation, for example, states that a worksheet in supported versions of Excel can contain up to 1,048,576 rows.

The practical distinction is how easily the business can manage its sales process with the tool.

A spreadsheet can store a follow-up date. A CRM may be able to create a task, notify the assigned salesperson, and show the manager which opportunities have not received attention. The exact capabilities depend on the system and how it is configured.

A CRM can also introduce new costs, implementation work, and training requirements. Its additional features only matter if the business needs them and can use them consistently.

## When a Spreadsheet Is Still Enough

A spreadsheet is not automatically an inferior choice. In some situations, it is the most practical tool available.

It may be sufficient when:

* One person manages most customer inquiries and sales opportunities.
* The number of active opportunities is manageable.
* The sales process has relatively few stages.
* Follow-up dates and next actions are easy to review.
* Customer interactions do not need to be recorded across several employees.
* Reporting requirements are simple.
* Records can be maintained accurately without excessive manual work.

Consider a consultant who receives a small number of inquiries each month. They record each prospect's name, service requirement, stage, next action, and expected decision date in a spreadsheet.

Every morning, they review upcoming follow-ups and update the status of each opportunity. Because they manage the entire process themselves, there may be little benefit in introducing a more complex system.

The spreadsheet is doing its job.

The consultant should not switch tools simply because a CRM appears more professional or because other businesses use one.

Instead, they should identify a specific limitation that justifies the cost and effort of changing.

### The condition that matters most

A spreadsheet remains useful only if the business can maintain it reliably.

If follow-up dates are frequently missed, different versions of the file circulate among team members, or nobody knows which records are current, the existing setup may no longer be adequate.

Even then, a CRM is not the only possible answer. Better spreadsheet design, clearer ownership, a shared file, and a consistent review process may resolve the problem.

The right intervention depends on the cause.

## Five Signs Your Business May Have Outgrown Its Spreadsheet

### 1. Follow-ups depend too heavily on memory

One of the clearest warning signs is a sales process in which the next action depends on someone remembering to take it.

A prospect requests a quotation. The business sends it. The owner becomes busy with delivery work and forgets to follow up. A week later, the prospect has chosen another provider or the opportunity has gone cold.

This may happen occasionally in any business. The concern is when it becomes a recurring pattern.

A spreadsheet can record follow-up dates, but someone must review the file and act on the information. A CRM may help by creating tasks and reminders, making overdue activities easier to identify.

**What to investigate:**

* How many active opportunities have no next action recorded?
* How often are planned follow-ups missed?
* Can you identify overdue opportunities without checking multiple files?
* Is there a clear person responsible for each opportunity?

If missed follow-ups are a real problem, first define the follow-up process. Then decide whether a CRM's reminders and task management would make that process more reliable.

Automating an unclear process will not make it effective.

### 2. Customer information is scattered across different places

A customer's information may be spread across email, messaging applications, spreadsheets, meeting notes, and individual employees' memories.

When the owner handles every conversation, this arrangement may be manageable. As more people become involved, it can become difficult to reconstruct the history of an opportunity.

A salesperson may not know which proposal was sent. A colleague may contact a prospect without knowing that another employee has already spoken with them. A manager may be unable to determine what was promised or agreed.

The problem is not that the business lacks data. It is that the data is difficult to access and use consistently.

**What to investigate:**

* Can an authorised team member quickly understand the history of an opportunity?
* Are duplicate customer records common?
* Do employees maintain separate customer lists?
* Are important decisions or commitments missing from the central record?
* Does the business depend on one employee to explain what happened with a prospect?

A CRM may provide a shared customer record and interaction history, depending on the communication channels and integrations in use.

However, the business must decide what information should be recorded and who is responsible for maintaining it. Software cannot automatically reconstruct information that was never captured.

### 3. Several people need to manage the same sales pipeline

A spreadsheet may work well when one person owns the entire process. Coordination becomes more complicated when several employees share responsibility for prospects and customers.

One employee may receive an inquiry, another may prepare a proposal, and a manager may approve pricing. Without clear ownership and shared records, the prospect can become stuck between people.

A CRM can make it easier to assign opportunities, record activities, and review responsibility. Some systems also support permissions, notifications, and team reporting.

But the tool is only part of the solution.

The business still needs clear rules about who owns an opportunity, when ownership changes, what information must be recorded, and what happens when an employee is unavailable.

**What to investigate:**

* Can every active opportunity be assigned to a responsible person?
* Do employees know who must take the next action?
* Are handovers between sales and delivery recorded?
* Can a manager see stalled opportunities without asking each employee for a separate update?

If coordination is the main problem, the business should assess whether a CRM can support a clearly defined process rather than expecting the software to create that process on its own.

### 4. You cannot reliably explain where sales are being lost

A customer list shows who has made contact. It does not necessarily show how opportunities move through the sales process.

If the business cannot determine how many inquiries become qualified opportunities, how many receive proposals, or how many become paying customers, it will struggle to identify where sales are being lost.

A spreadsheet can track these metrics. The challenge arises when the information requires repeated manual updates, inconsistent stage definitions, or time-consuming reporting.

A CRM may make pipeline reporting easier if its records are complete and its stages reflect real progress.

For example, a manager may discover that many qualified opportunities reach the proposal stage but few proceed to a sale. That observation provides a reason to investigate proposal quality, pricing concerns, decision timelines, or follow-up.

It does not prove which factor is responsible, but it helps narrow the investigation.

**What to investigate:**

* Can you calculate conversion rates between meaningful sales stages?
* Can you identify opportunities that have stalled?
* Can you distinguish new inquiries from qualified opportunities?
* Can you compare outcomes by service or acquisition channel?
* How much time does preparing a reliable pipeline report require?

If the business cannot answer these questions because its records are inconsistent, improving data quality should come before investing heavily in reporting features.

### 5. Manual administration is consuming too much time

As the sales process becomes more involved, employees may spend increasing amounts of time updating records, preparing reports, copying information between tools, and reminding one another about next steps.

Some manual work is necessary. The issue is whether the work is disproportionately consuming time that could be used for customer conversations, sales, or delivery.

A CRM may help automate repetitive activities, such as assigning tasks, updating opportunity stages through configured workflows, or sending internal notifications.

The potential benefit depends on how frequently the activity occurs, how much time it consumes, how error-prone it is, and whether the CRM can automate it appropriately.

Before buying software, estimate the actual burden.

For example, if two employees each spend three hours per week reconciling records and preparing pipeline updates, the business is using six staff-hours each week on that activity. This is a measurement of time, not proof that a CRM would eliminate all six hours.

The business should determine which tasks are avoidable, which can be simplified, and which could realistically be automated.

# How to Calculate the Cost of a Lost Sales Opportunity

A prospect requests a quotation, discusses their needs, and appears interested in your service. Your business invests time in the conversation, prepares a proposal, and follows up.

Then the prospect goes quiet, chooses another provider, postpones the decision, or decides not to proceed.

The opportunity disappears from the pipeline, and the business moves on to the next one.

But what did that lost opportunity actually cost?

Many businesses estimate the answer by adding up the potential value of every sale they did not win. That can make the problem appear larger than it is, because not every prospect would have purchased, and not every sale would have generated the same level of profit.

A more useful approach is to separate the possible revenue, the contribution the sale could have generated, the cost of pursuing the opportunity, and the extent to which the loss might have been preventable.

**The purpose of calculating lost-opportunity cost is not to turn every lost prospect into a financial loss on paper. It is to understand where the sales process may be wasting resources and where improvements could create measurable value.**

## What Is the Cost of a Lost Sales Opportunity?

A lost sales opportunity is a potential sale that does not proceed to a purchase within the relevant sales process.

The reasons vary. A prospect may choose a competitor, decide the service is unnecessary, lack the budget, postpone the decision, or discover that the offering is not a suitable fit.

The financial implications can include several different components:

* **Potential revenue:** The amount the business might have invoiced if the sale had occurred.
* **Potential contribution:** The revenue remaining after the relevant variable or direct costs of delivering the service.
* **Acquisition and sales costs:** The money and resources spent attracting, qualifying, and pursuing the opportunity.
* **Opportunity cost:** The productive time that could have been used for other activities.
* **Potential future value:** Additional contribution that a successful customer relationship might have generated, where there is credible evidence to estimate it.

These components should not be added together indiscriminately. Some overlap, and some are uncertain.

For example, if a business calculates potential contribution after direct delivery costs, it should not subtract those same delivery costs again when estimating the economic impact.

Likewise, the potential lifetime value of a customer should not be treated as guaranteed revenue. It depends on assumptions about repeat purchases, retention, margins, and future behaviour.

The first step is to decide which question the business needs to answer.

If the question is, “How much revenue did we fail to win?”, estimate potential revenue.

If the question is, “How much economic value might the sale have generated?”, estimate contribution and account for relevant additional costs.

If the question is, “Where should we improve our sales process?”, examine the causes of lost opportunities and the cost of pursuing them.

## Step 1: Estimate the Potential Revenue

Start with the value of the proposed sale.

For a business that sells a clearly priced service, this may be the quoted fee. For recurring services, it may be the expected contract value over a defined period.

Suppose a consulting firm loses a proposed engagement worth US$2,000 (₦2,700,000). The potential revenue associated with the opportunity is US$2,000 (₦2,700,000).

That figure is useful, but it does not mean the business has lost US$2,000 (₦2,700,000) in profit.

The engagement would have required time and resources to deliver. The prospect might also have rejected the proposal regardless of what the business did.

The quoted amount represents the potential revenue attached to the opportunity, not the amount the business can assume it would have kept.

For a recurring contract, be clear about the period being measured. A contract worth US$1,000 (₦1,350,000) per month could have a value of US$12,000 (₦16,200,000) over twelve months if it remained active for the entire year. But the full twelve-month amount is not guaranteed merely because the prospect expressed interest.

Use a defined and realistic period rather than choosing whichever figure makes the opportunity appear largest.

## Step 2: Calculate the Contribution the Sale Could Have Generated

Revenue is not the same as contribution.

To estimate contribution, subtract the relevant direct or variable costs required to deliver the service from its selling price.

Consider a service business that quotes US$2,000 (₦2,700,000) for a project and estimates that direct delivery costs would be US$1,200 (₦1,620,000).

The potential contribution would be:

| Item                            |                Amount |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------: |
| Potential project revenue       | US$2,000 (₦2,700,000) |
| Estimated direct delivery costs | US$1,200 (₦1,620,000) |
| Potential contribution          |   US$800 (₦1,080,000) |

*Currency note: Naira equivalents use the illustrative reference rate of US$1 = ₦1,350, dated 14 October 2026, as specified for these examples. Exchange rates vary by source, transaction type, and date. These conversions are illustrative, not guaranteed transaction rates.*

The estimated contribution is US$800 (₦1,080,000), not US$2,000 (₦2,700,000).

It is also not necessarily net profit. The business may still need to cover fixed operating expenses and other costs not included in the direct delivery estimate.

This distinction matters when assessing sales performance. A business that loses a high-value but low-contribution project may have a different commercial problem from one that repeatedly loses profitable engagements.

It is also possible that a sale should not have been pursued at the quoted price. If the work would have generated inadequate contribution or consumed capacity needed for more profitable projects, winning the sale might not have improved the business.

The goal is not to win every opportunity. It is to win suitable opportunities on commercially sound terms.

## Step 3: Account for the Cost of Pursuing the Opportunity

A lost opportunity can consume resources even when no sale occurs.

The business may have paid for advertising, spent time qualifying the prospect, held meetings, prepared a proposal, arranged a site visit, or involved technical staff in estimating the work.

Some of these costs are shared across multiple prospects. Others are specific to an individual opportunity.

For a practical estimate, begin with the costs that can reasonably be attributed to the opportunity.

For example:

| Sales activity                                         | Illustrative cost |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------: |
| Allocated acquisition cost                             |  US$80 (₦108,000) |
| Staff time spent qualifying and discussing the project |   US$60 (₦81,000) |
| Proposal preparation and technical review              | US$100 (₦135,000) |
| Total estimated pursuit cost                           | US$240 (₦324,000) |

These figures are illustrative. The business would need to estimate its own acquisition costs and the economic cost of staff time.

The total of US$240 (₦324,000) is the estimated cost of pursuing the opportunity, not an additional amount of lost revenue.

The business should not automatically add it to the potential contribution and label the result “lost profit.” That would mix different concepts.

Instead, the pursuit cost answers a separate question: how much resource did the business invest in trying to win this opportunity?

If the same type of opportunity is repeatedly lost after extensive proposal work, the business may need to investigate qualification, pricing, proposal effort, or the buying process.

## Step 4: Estimate the Impact Across Multiple Opportunities

A single lost sale does not always reveal a pattern. Reviewing a group of opportunities can help the business understand the scale of the problem.

Suppose a service business records 20 lost opportunities during a defined period. Each had an estimated average potential contribution of US$500 (₦675,000).

The combined potential contribution attached to those opportunities would be US$10,000 (₦13,500,000).

That is a useful measure of the potential economic value associated with the lost opportunities. But it is not a reliable estimate of the contribution the business could actually have recovered.

Some prospects may never have purchased. Others may have been unsuitable, unable to afford the service, or unlikely to proceed regardless of follow-up.

The business therefore needs to investigate the reasons for the losses and estimate what proportion, if any, may have been preventable.

For example, suppose the business has credible evidence that a recurring follow-up problem affected a subset of lost opportunities. It should focus its analysis on that subset rather than assume that every lost opportunity could have been recovered.

The question becomes:

**How much contribution might the business reasonably recover by correcting a specific, evidenced problem?**

That is a more useful basis for deciding whether a sales-process improvement is worth pursuing.

## Step 5: Distinguish Preventable Losses from Normal Sales Losses

Not every lost opportunity signals a failure.

A prospect may choose another provider because that provider offers a service that better fits their requirements. A customer may postpone a project because its priorities have changed. A business may disqualify an opportunity because the prospect is not a suitable fit.

These are normal outcomes in a sales process.

The more useful task is to identify recurring losses associated with problems the business can reasonably influence.

Examples include:

* Qualified inquiries that receive no response.
* Proposals sent without confirming the prospect's needs.
* Opportunities that stall because nobody owns the next action.
* Repeated objections that the business has not investigated.
* Prospects attracted by an offer that does not match the service actually delivered.
* Excessive time spent preparing proposals for poorly qualified opportunities.

Record the reason for each loss where possible. If the reason is unknown, mark it as unknown rather than inventing an explanation.

Then review the patterns.

If many opportunities are lost because the service is unsuitable, the business may need better targeting or qualification. If prospects repeatedly withdraw after receiving proposals, the business may need to examine the offer, scope, pricing, or how value is communicated. If follow-ups are missed, ownership and process discipline may be the relevant issue.

The cause should determine the intervention.

## A Worked Example: What Might a Sales Improvement Be Worth?

Consider a service business that reviews 40 lost opportunities.

Its records suggest that a subset of 10 qualified opportunities were not followed up consistently. Each had an estimated potential contribution of US$600 (₦810,000).

The combined potential contribution associated with those ten opportunities is US$6,000 (₦8,100,000).

However, the business should not assume it could recover the full amount.

Suppose, for planning purposes only, the business estimates that a targeted follow-up improvement might recover two of those ten opportunities. If each recovered sale generated the estimated contribution of US$600 (₦810,000), the potential contribution recovered would be US$1,200 (₦1,620,000).

That is an illustrative scenario, not a forecast or a measured recovery rate.

The business would need to validate whether the original losses were genuinely related to follow-up, whether the prospects remain viable, whether the contribution estimates are realistic, and what it would cost to implement and maintain the improvement.

If the change requires additional staff time or software, those costs should be considered separately when evaluating the economics.

The value of this exercise is that it links a specific business problem to a measurable hypothesis. Rather than investing in a broad sales transformation, the business can test whether a focused intervention improves outcomes.

## How to Build a Lost-Opportunity Review

A spreadsheet may be sufficient to start. Record each lost opportunity using a consistent set of fields:

| Field                         | Why it matters                                                           |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Opportunity value             | Estimates the potential revenue attached to the sale                     |
| Estimated contribution        | Shows the potential value after relevant direct delivery costs           |
| Acquisition source            | Helps compare the commercial outcomes of different channels              |
| Sales stage at loss           | Identifies where opportunities tend to stop progressing                  |
| Recorded loss reason          | Helps identify recurring problems                                        |
| Pursuit cost, where available | Estimates the resources spent trying to win the opportunity              |
| Follow-up history             | Helps establish whether the sales process was followed                   |
| Potential next action         | Identifies whether further investigation or a targeted test is justified |

Keep the definitions consistent. For example, do not record one opportunity's full multi-year contract value while recording another's first-month revenue without making the difference explicit.

Also avoid treating every estimate as a fact. If delivery costs, pursuit costs, or reasons for loss are uncertain, record that uncertainty.

Review the information periodically, looking for patterns across acquisition channels, service types, customer segments, and pipeline stages. Small samples may be useful for generating questions, but they may not support strong conclusions.

## What Should You Fix First?

The largest opportunity by quoted value is not necessarily the best place to start.

A high-value prospect may have been unlikely to buy. A smaller opportunity may reveal a recurring process problem affecting many sales.

Prioritise issues using four considerations:

1. **Frequency:** How often does the problem occur?
2. **Commercial impact:** What contribution or resources may be affected?
3. **Preventability:** Is there credible evidence that the business can influence the outcome?
4. **Cost of correction:** What time, money, and operational effort would a solution require?

For example, improving follow-up consistency may be a sensible test if records show that qualified prospects regularly receive no follow-up. But buying a CRM would not automatically be justified if the same problem could be solved with clearer responsibility and a simple task-review process.

Likewise, lowering prices may not recover lost opportunities if prospects are uncertain about the service's value or if the offering is unsuitable for their needs.

Do not choose an intervention merely because it is easy to implement or because it is the tool the business already wants to buy. Choose it because the evidence supports the problem being addressed.

## The Goal Is Better Sales Decisions, Not Inflated Loss Estimates

Calculating the cost of lost sales opportunities can help a business understand what it is missing, but the figures must be interpreted carefully.

Potential revenue is not guaranteed revenue. Potential contribution is not net profit. The combined value of lost opportunities is not the amount a business can necessarily recover. And a prospect who did not buy is not automatically evidence that the sales process failed.

The most useful analysis connects the financial value of an opportunity with the reason it was lost and the likelihood that a specific improvement could change the outcome.

Start with reliable records. Separate revenue from contribution. Estimate the cost of pursuing opportunities where practical. Identify recurring, potentially preventable problems. Then test focused improvements and measure what changes.

# Why Customer Retention Matters More Than Just Winning the Next Sale

**Category:** Strengthening Client Retention

Winning a new customer is an important business achievement. But what happens after the first sale?

Does the customer return? Do they renew their contract, book another appointment, purchase another project, or recommend your business to someone else? Or does the relationship end after one transaction, leaving you to find another customer to replace the revenue?

For many service businesses, growth is measured by the number of new customers acquired. Marketing campaigns are reviewed, inquiries are tracked, and sales targets are set. Yet the business may pay less attention to what happens to customers after they have bought.

That creates a potential weakness in the revenue system.

**Customer retention is not simply about keeping customers happy. It is about understanding whether your business can continue delivering value, maintain profitable relationships, and earn repeat business over time.**

The objective is not to stop acquiring new customers. It is to understand how acquisition and retention work together, and whether the business is losing opportunities by focusing on one while neglecting the other.

## 1. Why the First Sale Is Only Part of the Picture

Acquiring a customer takes resources. Depending on the business, those resources may include advertising, sales time, referrals, consultations, proposal preparation, and other activities required to win the customer's business.

Once a customer has purchased, the business has an opportunity to build on the relationship. The customer may return for another service, renew an agreement, buy a complementary service, or refer someone else.

These outcomes are not automatic. They depend on factors such as service quality, customer needs, pricing, trust, convenience, and whether the business maintains a useful relationship after the sale.

Research published by Harvard Business Review has discussed the economic value of customer retention, while also highlighting the importance of keeping the *right* customers. The commercial case is not that every existing customer should be retained at any cost. It is that customer relationships should be evaluated over time rather than solely by the first transaction.

Source: [Harvard Business Review, *The Value of Keeping the Right Customers*](https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers).

Consider a consulting practice that spends time and money attracting a new client for a single project. If the client receives a good result and later needs related support, the practice may be able to serve that client again without repeating every step of the original sales process.

However, the second project still has delivery costs. The client may require additional support, negotiate a lower price, or purchase a service with a smaller margin. Repeat business is an opportunity, not proof of higher profitability.

This is why businesses need to look beyond the number of customers acquired.

They need to understand how customer relationships contribute to revenue, costs, and future opportunities.

## 2. What Poor Customer Retention Can Cost a Business

When customers do not return, a business may need to replace them with new customers simply to maintain its existing level of activity.

That can create several commercial pressures.

### More pressure on customer acquisition

If a business repeatedly loses customers after their first purchase, it may need a steady supply of new customers to sustain revenue.

This does not automatically mean its marketing is ineffective. Some services are naturally purchased only once, and some customers have no further need for the service.

The question is whether customers who could reasonably return are leaving because the business has failed to meet their needs, maintain contact, or make the next purchase straightforward.

### Less predictable revenue

Businesses with repeat-purchase relationships, renewals, or ongoing service agreements may be able to plan more confidently when those relationships are healthy.

When repeat business is inconsistent, future revenue may depend more heavily on winning new projects or customers.

The effect will differ by business model. A dental clinic, a one-off event planner, a business consultant, and a commercial contractor do not have the same natural purchase cycles. Retention should therefore be measured against the way customers actually buy.

### Lost opportunities to expand a relationship

A customer who trusts a business may eventually need another service, a larger project, maintenance, or ongoing support.

If the business never reviews changing customer needs, it may miss relevant opportunities to help existing customers.

This does not mean pushing additional services on every customer. The business should identify genuine needs and offer an appropriate solution when one exists.

### Fewer opportunities for referrals

Satisfied customers may introduce a business to other potential customers. However, satisfaction does not guarantee that a referral will happen.

Businesses need to understand whether referrals occur, where they originate, and which customer relationships generate them.

A September–October 2026 Harvard Business Review article by Fred Reichheld, Jamie Cleghorn, and Wojtek Kokoszka examined the role of customer referrals in business growth. Its discussion reinforces the value of tracking referral behaviour instead of assuming that positive customer experiences will automatically produce new business.

Source: [Harvard Business Review, *Don't Underestimate the Power of Customer Referrals*](https://hbr.org/2026/09/dont-underestimate-the-power-of-customer-referrals).

The practical lesson is straightforward: customer retention can affect more than repeat sales. It can also influence revenue stability, the need for replacement customers, and the opportunities generated by existing relationships.

## 3. Why Customer Retention Does Not Automatically Mean Higher Profit

Retention is valuable only when the economics of the relationship make sense.

A business can retain a customer for years and still earn little profit from that relationship. The customer may require excessive support, repeatedly request discounts, delay payment, or purchase services that are expensive to deliver.

Conversely, a customer who buys infrequently may still be commercially valuable if each project produces a healthy contribution and requires relatively little acquisition effort.

Research on customer profitability has warned against treating loyalty as a reliable substitute for financial analysis. A Harvard Business Review research summary indexed by PubMed notes that some loyal customers are unprofitable, while some customers who are not especially loyal can still be highly profitable.

Source: [PubMed, *Getting the Most Out of All Your Customers*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15241958/).

This distinction matters because a retention strategy can fail when it focuses on keeping every customer rather than understanding which relationships create sustainable value.

### An illustrative example

Imagine a professional service business that earns the following from two customer relationships.

| Measure                             |            Customer A |            Customer B |
| ----------------------------------- | --------------------: | --------------------: |
| Annual revenue                      | US$2,000 (₦2,700,000) | US$2,000 (₦2,700,000) |
| Direct delivery costs               | US$1,000 (₦1,350,000) | US$1,600 (₦2,160,000) |
| Contribution before other overheads | US$1,000 (₦1,350,000) |     US$400 (₦540,000) |
| Contribution margin                 |                   50% |                   20% |

*Illustrative example. These are hypothetical figures, not industry benchmarks.*

Both customers generate the same revenue, but their direct delivery economics differ.

Customer A generates US$1,000 (₦1,350,000) in contribution before other overheads. Customer B generates US$400 (₦540,000).

If retaining Customer B requires substantial additional support or repeated discounts, the business should examine whether the relationship remains commercially sensible and whether the service arrangement needs to change.

That does not mean immediately dropping Customer B. The business might be able to improve the scope, pricing, delivery process, or communication.

The point is that **retention should be evaluated alongside profitability, not treated as an objective in isolation.**

*Currency note: Naira equivalents use an illustrative reference rate of US$1 = ₦1,350, dated 14 October 2026, as specified for these examples. Exchange rates vary by source, transaction type, and date. These conversions are illustrative, not guaranteed transaction rates.*

## 4. Four Questions to Ask About Your Existing Customers

Before investing in a new retention campaign, investigate what is happening within the current customer base.

### Question 1: Are customers returning when they reasonably should?

The answer depends on the service.

A customer who books a routine appointment may have a predictable reason to return. A company that commissions a website may not need another website for several years. A commercial contractor may win repeat work when new projects arise, but there may be no immediate reason for a previous client to buy again.

Start by defining the expected customer behaviour.

For example:

* For a dental clinic, examine whether patients return for recommended follow-up care, while accounting for the type of treatment and appropriate clinical guidance.
* For a consulting practice, examine renewals, follow-on engagements, and whether clients return when relevant needs arise.
* For a maintenance provider, examine contract renewals, scheduled servicing, and repeat service requests.
* For a professional services firm, examine repeat projects, ongoing engagements, and the time between purchases.

Do not label a customer as lost simply because they have not purchased again within an arbitrary period. Their need may not have arisen yet.

Instead, define a reasonable observation period based on the actual buying cycle.

### Question 2: Why do customers stop buying?

A decline in repeat business is a signal to investigate, not a diagnosis by itself.

Possible explanations include:

* The customer no longer needs the service.
* The original problem was not resolved satisfactorily.
* Communication after delivery was poor.
* The customer found a more suitable alternative.
* Pricing or payment terms no longer fit the customer's circumstances.
* The business failed to explain relevant next steps.
* The customer has not been contacted when a useful follow-up would have been appropriate.
* The service was designed for a one-off transaction rather than an ongoing relationship.

These causes require different responses.

If customers leave because their needs have changed, more frequent marketing messages may accomplish little. If customers leave because the service was unreliable, a promotional campaign will not fix the underlying delivery problem.

Where practical, review lost-customer records, feedback, complaints, service history, and conversations with former customers.

Ask neutral questions such as:

* What influenced your decision not to continue?
* Was there anything about the service that did not meet your expectations?
* What would have made the experience more useful?
* Have your needs changed since we last worked together?

The purpose is to discover patterns, not to pressure customers into returning.

### Question 3: Which customers generate sustainable value?

Not every customer relationship needs the same retention approach.

Some customers purchase frequently but require significant support. Others purchase less often but generate strong margins, pay reliably, or refer suitable new customers.

Segment customers using relevant commercial and service information, such as:

* Purchase frequency or contract renewal history.
* Revenue over a defined period.
* Contribution after direct delivery costs.
* Payment reliability.
* Support requirements.
* Customer satisfaction and unresolved issues.
* Referrals and relevant opportunities generated.

Be careful with estimated customer lifetime value. A projection of future revenue is not the same as realised profit. It should account for delivery costs, retention expenses, uncertainty, and the period being considered.

The goal is not to label customers as important or unimportant based on one metric. It is to understand which relationships warrant additional attention and what kind of attention is appropriate.

### Question 4: Is there a clear reason and process for staying in touch?

A relationship can weaken when communication stops after the sale.

However, contacting customers more often is not automatically better. Messages should be relevant, timely, and appropriate to the relationship.

Useful follow-up may include:

* Checking whether the delivered service met the customer's expectations.
* Confirming outstanding implementation or support needs.
* Providing information relevant to a service the customer already uses.
* Reminding customers about a scheduled renewal or agreed review.
* Explaining a useful next step when there is a genuine reason to do so.
* Asking for feedback after a meaningful interaction.

For example, a business that completes a consulting engagement could schedule a review of the agreed outcomes. That conversation may reveal a new problem, confirm that no further work is required, or identify a suitable follow-on engagement.

The process should serve the customer's needs. Any additional sale should follow from a genuine fit, not from a predetermined requirement to sell again.

## 5. The Customer Retention Metrics Worth Tracking

A business does not need a complicated analytics platform to begin understanding retention. It needs reliable customer records, clear definitions, and consistent measurement.

Start with a small set of metrics that reflect the business model.

### Customer retention rate

Retention rate measures the share of customers present at the start of a period who remain customers at the end of that period.

A common calculation is:

**Customer retention rate = (Customers at the end of the period − New customers acquired during the period) ÷ Customers at the start of the period × 100**

For example, suppose a business starts the quarter with 100 customers. During the quarter, it acquires 20 new customers and ends with 85 customers.

Its retention rate is:

(85 − 20) ÷ 100 × 100 = **65%**

This example assumes the business uses a consistent definition of an active customer and that the customers acquired during the quarter are counted as new customers.

The result means that 65 of the original 100 customers remained. It does not explain why the other 35 left or whether their departure was avoidable.

### Repeat-customer rate

This measures the proportion of customers who purchase more than once within a defined period.

**Repeat-customer rate = Customers who purchased more than once ÷ Total unique customers in the period × 100**

For example, if 40 out of 100 unique customers made at least two purchases during the defined period, the repeat-customer rate would be 40%.

This metric is more useful for businesses where repeat purchases are a meaningful part of the model. It may be less informative for a business whose service is normally purchased only once.

### Revenue from existing customers

Track how much revenue comes from existing customers during a defined period.

This can help reveal whether the business is generating more activity from established relationships or depending increasingly on newly acquired customers.

Revenue alone is insufficient, however. If repeat sales require deep discounts or costly delivery, higher repeat revenue may not produce a corresponding improvement in profit.

### Contribution from existing customers

Where the necessary data is available, calculate the contribution generated by existing customers after direct delivery costs.

This provides a more useful commercial view than revenue alone.

A customer who generates US$5,000 (₦6,750,000) in revenue but requires US$4,500 (₦6,075,000) in direct delivery costs contributes US$500 (₦675,000) before other overheads. A smaller relationship could contribute more if its costs are lower.

### Customer loss and reasons for leaving

Record how many customers stop buying, fail to renew, or become inactive within a relevant period. Where possible, record the reason.

The most useful insight may not be the overall loss rate. It may be a recurring pattern within a particular customer segment, service line, or stage of the customer experience.

**Do not compare retention rates blindly across industries.** A subscription business, a dental clinic, a consultancy, and a one-off project provider have different customer lifecycles. Compare your results with your own history and the buying patterns that make sense for your service.

## 6. A Practical Way to Improve Retention

Once the business understands its customer patterns, it can decide what to change.

Use this five-step process.

**Step 1: Establish a baseline.**

Choose a relevant period and record the number of active customers, new customers, returning customers, lost customers, and revenue from existing customers. Where possible, include contribution after direct delivery costs.

**Step 2: Identify the most important pattern.**

Look for evidence of a specific problem. Are customers leaving after a poor service experience? Are renewal conversations starting too late? Are customers failing to return because there is no suitable follow-up process? Or are repeat purchases simply uncommon for this type of service?

**Step 3: Investigate the cause.**

Review customer feedback, service records, payment history, communication, and the timing of repeat purchases. Speak with customers where appropriate.

Do not assume that a low retention rate proves the business needs more emails, discounts, or loyalty rewards.

**Step 4: Make one targeted change.**

Examples include improving the post-service review process, resolving a recurring delivery problem, clarifying renewal terms, assigning responsibility for customer follow-up, or creating a relevant reminder for customers who may need another service.

Choose an intervention that addresses the suspected cause.

**Step 5: Measure the result.**

Track the relevant outcome over a suitable period. Depending on the intervention, that may be repeat purchase rate, renewal rate, customer loss, contribution from existing customers, or the number of customers who respond to an appropriate follow-up.

Compare the result with the baseline, while considering other factors that may have influenced it. One improvement in a small customer group does not automatically establish that the change caused the result.

Continue, adjust, or stop the intervention based on the evidence.

## 7. What Could Better Retention Mean Financially?

Consider a hypothetical service business with 100 existing customers.

Suppose 30 of those customers purchase an additional service during the next year. The business earns an average contribution of US$300 (₦405,000) from each additional purchase, after direct delivery costs but before other overheads.

The contribution from those repeat purchases is:

30 × US$300 = **US$9,000 (₦12,150,000)**.

Now suppose the business identifies a service or communication problem and tests a change. In a later comparable period, 35 customers make an additional purchase, with the same average contribution.

The contribution becomes:

35 × US$300 = **US$10,500 (₦14,175,000)**.

The difference is US$1,500 (₦2,025,000) in additional contribution before the costs of the retention initiative and other overheads.

*This is an illustrative calculation, not a forecast or a claim that a retention campaign will produce five additional customers. The example assumes the same customer base, average contribution, and measurement period. Actual results may differ.*

The business should subtract the relevant costs of implementing the change before assessing its net financial impact. It should also consider whether the additional purchases displaced other work, affected capacity, or required extra support.

The calculation demonstrates why retention deserves attention: even a modest change in repeat purchasing can have a measurable financial effect. But the business must measure the outcome rather than assume that a campaign caused it.

## 8. When Should a Business Prioritise Retention?

Retention deserves closer investigation when:

* Existing customers disappear without clear reasons.
* Repeat business is lower than the business model would reasonably suggest.
* Customer complaints or service problems recur.
* Revenue depends heavily on continually finding replacement customers.
* Renewals or follow-up opportunities are missed.
* The business has customer records but rarely uses them to guide decisions.
* Existing customers have relevant needs that the business is equipped to serve but does not systematically identify.

Acquisition still matters. A business entering a new market, launching a new service, or expanding its customer base may need to invest heavily in winning new customers.

The right balance depends on the business's goals, customer lifecycle, margins, capacity, and evidence about where opportunities are being lost.

The decision should not be framed as retention versus acquisition. It should be framed as **where the next investment is most likely to improve sustainable business performance**.

## The Bottom Line

Winning new customers creates opportunities for growth. Retaining suitable customers can help a business build on those relationships, generate repeat business, and reduce the need to replace every customer through new acquisition.

But retention is not a goal to pursue blindly. Some customers will not need the service again, some relationships will no longer be commercially suitable, and some businesses naturally operate through one-off transactions.

The important questions are whether customers return when it makes sense, whether the business understands why relationships end, and whether existing customer relationships contribute to sustainable profit.

Before investing more in customer acquisition, examine what happens after the first sale. You may discover that the next growth opportunity lies in improving the experience, follow-up, or service process for customers you have already won.

# How to Build a Follow-Up System That Generates Repeat Business

**Category:** Strengthening Client Retention

A customer buys from your business. You deliver the service, receive payment, and move on to the next customer.

Weeks or months later, the customer may need your service again. But by then, they may have forgotten about your business, found another provider, or assumed that the relationship ended with the original transaction.

The problem may not be the quality of your service. It may be that your business has no deliberate process for maintaining the relationship.

This is common in businesses where customer follow-up depends on memory. The owner intends to check in with previous customers but becomes occupied with new inquiries, ongoing projects, and daily operations. Some customers receive excellent follow-up, while others hear nothing until they make contact again.

**A follow-up system replaces inconsistent intentions with a repeatable process for contacting the right customer, at the right time, for a relevant reason.**

The objective is not to send more messages. It is to identify useful opportunities to support customers, maintain relationships, and generate repeat business when there is a genuine need.

## 1. Why Customer Follow-Up Needs a System

Customer relationships do not always end when a transaction is complete.

A consulting client may need further advice after implementing a recommendation. A dental patient may require an appropriate follow-up appointment. A commercial property may need scheduled maintenance. A business that has purchased a website may later need updates, technical support, or a new feature.

These opportunities differ by business, but they share one important characteristic: **the next interaction may need to be initiated deliberately.**

Salesforce's customer relationship guidance recommends continued engagement after a sale, including gathering feedback, providing support, reviewing customer needs, and identifying suitable future opportunities. This is guidance from a company that sells customer relationship management software, rather than independent evidence that a particular follow-up schedule will increase sales for every business.

The practical principle remains useful: the relationship should not be managed only when the customer contacts the business.

Source: Salesforce, *Retaining and Growing Customers*.

Without a system, businesses may experience several problems:

* Customers who need another service are forgotten.
* Renewal dates arrive without sufficient preparation.
* Problems after delivery remain unresolved.
* Customer feedback is collected inconsistently.
* Follow-up responsibilities are unclear.
* The business cannot determine whether its communication generates useful outcomes.

These are not necessarily marketing problems. Some may originate in service delivery, customer experience, account management, or internal operations.

Before adding more messages or buying automation software, determine what the business needs its follow-up process to accomplish.

## 2. Start With the Reason for Following Up

A weak follow-up system begins with a schedule: send a message after seven days, another after fourteen days, and another after thirty days.

Those intervals may be suitable for a particular situation, but they are not universal rules.

A more effective starting point is the customer's situation.

Ask three questions:

1. What has happened in the customer relationship?
2. What might the customer reasonably need next?
3. What action should the business take, if any?

The answers determine the appropriate follow-up.

### After a service has been delivered

The purpose may be to confirm that the work met expectations, answer outstanding questions, or resolve a problem.

For example, a web development business might contact a client after launch to check that the website is functioning as expected and that the client understands the agreed handover process.

The immediate objective is service quality, not an additional sale.

### When a customer may need the service again

The purpose may be to remind the customer about a relevant next step.

A maintenance provider, for instance, might contact a client before a scheduled service is due. The timing should reflect the service agreement or actual maintenance requirements, not an arbitrary marketing calendar.

### Before a contract or agreement expires

The purpose may be to give the customer enough time to review renewal terms, ask questions, and make a decision.

A renewal process should begin early enough to address potential obstacles. The appropriate lead time depends on the contract, purchasing process, and complexity of the service.

### When a customer has become inactive

The purpose is to understand whether the relationship still has potential.

A customer who has not purchased recently may no longer need the service. Another may have an unresolved issue or simply have forgotten to return.

The business should use what it knows about the customer's previous activity to decide whether contact is appropriate.

### When a relevant new opportunity arises

The purpose may be to inform a customer about a service or development that addresses a genuine need.

This is different from sending every customer the same promotion. The message should explain why the information is relevant to that particular relationship.

**The rule is simple: every follow-up should have a clear purpose that makes sense from the customer's perspective.**

## 3. Build a Simple Follow-Up Workflow

A useful system does not need to begin with complex automation. It needs a reliable sequence of steps.

### Step 1: Record the customer and the interaction

Keep a central record of the customer, the service purchased, the date of the interaction, and any relevant commitments.

Depending on the business, the record might also include:

* Customer contact details and preferred communication channel.
* Service or project delivered.
* Outstanding issues or agreed next steps.
* Relevant renewal or review dates.
* Previous follow-up attempts.
* Customer response and outcome.
* The person responsible for the next action.

Collect only the information needed to manage the relationship, and handle customer information in accordance with applicable privacy and consent requirements.

A spreadsheet may be sufficient for a small business with a manageable number of customers. A CRM may become useful when multiple employees need access to customer history, follow-up tasks, reminders, and reporting.

The objective is reliable visibility, not the purchase of a particular tool.

### Step 2: Define the follow-up trigger

A trigger is the event or condition that tells the business when a follow-up action should be considered.

Examples include:

| Trigger                                     | Possible follow-up action                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Service completed                           | Check that the agreed outcome was delivered and address outstanding issues  |
| Customer feedback received                  | Assign responsibility for resolving the issue or acknowledging the feedback |
| Scheduled maintenance approaching           | Send a relevant reminder                                                    |
| Contract renewal approaching                | Begin the renewal conversation                                              |
| Agreed customer review date reached         | Review results, changing needs, and next steps                              |
| Previously active customer becomes inactive | Assess whether a relevant re-engagement message is appropriate              |

The trigger should reflect the service and customer relationship.

For example, a reminder for a scheduled maintenance visit may be appropriate because the service has a known interval. A reminder to purchase another website after thirty days would be arbitrary for most customers.

### Step 3: Assign ownership

Every follow-up task needs a responsible person.

If responsibility is shared vaguely across a team, employees may assume someone else has contacted the customer. If the owner manages every relationship personally, follow-up may become inconsistent as the business grows.

For each task, specify:

* Who is responsible?
* When should the action happen?
* What should the action accomplish?
* What should happen if the customer does not respond?
* Where will the outcome be recorded?

A named owner and a due date turn follow-up into a manageable business process.

### Step 4: Use relevant communication

The message should refer to the customer's situation and make the next step clear.

A useful follow-up generally explains why the business is contacting the customer, provides relevant information, and offers a reasonable next action.

Avoid messages that merely ask whether the customer is ready to buy again.

For example, after delivering a consulting engagement, a business might ask whether the client has been able to implement the agreed recommendations and whether any issues need clarification.

That conversation can reveal whether the customer needs additional help. It can also establish that the original engagement is complete and no further work is necessary.

Both outcomes are useful.

### Step 5: Record the result and decide what happens next

A follow-up is not complete merely because a message was sent.

Record whether the customer responded, whether an issue was resolved, whether a meeting was booked, whether a renewal progressed, or whether the opportunity ended.

If the customer does not respond, follow the business's agreed policy for further contact. Do not continue messaging indefinitely.

The process should also allow the business to stop or adjust communication when a customer declines further contact, no longer has a relevant need, or should not be contacted for another reason.

This creates a feedback loop: each interaction informs the next appropriate action.

## 4. How Often Should You Follow Up?

There is no single follow-up frequency that works for every service business.

The right timing depends on the purpose of the contact, the customer's buying cycle, the service agreement, the urgency of the issue, and the customer's communication preferences.

Consider three examples.

**A dental clinic:** Follow-up should reflect the patient's care plan and appropriate clinical guidance. Administrative reminders may help patients attend scheduled appointments, but the clinic should not treat every patient as if they require the same appointment schedule.

**A consulting practice:** A post-engagement review may be useful after the client has had time to implement recommendations. A further discussion can then focus on results, unresolved challenges, or emerging needs.

**A commercial maintenance provider:** Follow-up can be tied to contract milestones, scheduled maintenance, outstanding work, or renewal dates. The schedule should be based on the agreement and the operational requirements of the service.

In each case, the timing follows the relationship rather than an arbitrary marketing rule.

Businesses should also distinguish between essential service communication and promotional communication. A message about an unresolved issue is different from a sales promotion, and the appropriate handling may differ.

## 5. A Practical Follow-Up System You Can Implement This Week

A small service business can start with a simple spreadsheet or customer record system.

Create a table with these fields:

| Field              | Purpose                                                              |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Customer           | Identifies the customer                                              |
| Service or project | Shows what the customer purchased                                    |
| Last interaction   | Records the most recent relevant contact                             |
| Follow-up reason   | Explains why contact is appropriate                                  |
| Next action        | Defines what needs to happen                                         |
| Due date           | Sets when the task should be completed                               |
| Owner              | Assigns responsibility                                               |
| Status             | Shows whether the task is pending, completed, or awaiting a response |
| Outcome            | Records what happened                                                |
| Next review date   | Identifies when another action may be appropriate                    |

Then follow this implementation sequence.

**Day 1: Review existing customer records.**

Identify recent customers, outstanding commitments, upcoming renewals, unresolved issues, and customers with relevant next steps.

Do not contact everyone simply because their names appear in the database.

**Day 2: Identify the most important follow-up opportunities.**

Prioritise overdue commitments, unresolved service issues, and customers with a clear reason for contact.

**Day 3: Define the process.**

For each type of follow-up, specify the trigger, responsible person, expected timing, message purpose, and required next step.

**Day 4: Prepare message templates.**

Create a small number of templates for recurring situations, such as a post-service check-in, a scheduled maintenance reminder, or a renewal conversation.

Templates should reduce repetitive work without making communication impersonal. Employees should adapt them to the actual customer and situation.

**Day 5: Begin tracking outcomes.**

Record completed follow-ups, customer responses, unresolved issues, appointments booked, repeat purchases, and renewals where relevant.

Review the process weekly at first. Look for missed tasks, irrelevant messages, unclear ownership, and recurring customer needs.

This is enough to establish a basic system before introducing more sophisticated automation.

## 6. When Should You Automate Follow-Up?

Automation can help when the process is already understood and contains repetitive, predictable tasks.

For example, a business may automate reminders for upcoming appointments, create a task when a contract approaches renewal, or notify an employee when a customer has not received an agreed response.

A CRM can help centralise customer history, assign ownership, and make overdue tasks visible. Depending on the platform and configuration, it may also trigger messages or workflows based on customer activity.

However, automation does not correct a poorly designed process by itself.

If the business does not know why it contacts customers, which customers should receive which messages, or what outcome it wants to measure, automation may simply deliver irrelevant messages faster.

Before automating, confirm that:

* The follow-up trigger is clear.
* Customer records are sufficiently accurate.
* Responsibility and escalation rules are defined.
* Messages are appropriate to the relationship.
* Consent, opt-out, and privacy requirements are respected.
* The business can measure the outcome.

Start with one workflow, test it, and correct problems before expanding.

## 7. How Do You Know Whether Follow-Up Is Working?

The number of messages sent is an activity measure. It does not establish that the system is creating value.

Track outcomes that correspond to the purpose of the follow-up.

Useful measures include:

* **Follow-up completion rate:** the percentage of follow-up tasks completed by their due dates.
* **Response rate:** the percentage of contacted customers who respond, using a consistent definition of a response.
* **Issue resolution rate:** the percentage of follow-up cases where the identified issue is resolved.
* **Appointment or meeting rate:** the percentage of relevant follow-ups that result in a scheduled appointment or meeting.
* **Renewal rate:** the proportion of eligible customers who renew within the defined period.
* **Repeat-purchase rate:** the proportion of customers who make another purchase during the relevant period.
* **Contribution from repeat business:** the contribution generated by repeat purchases after direct delivery costs, before other overheads.

Choose metrics that fit the workflow. A post-service quality check should not be judged only by immediate sales, while a renewal process should track renewal outcomes.

### An illustrative financial example

Suppose a service business identifies 40 existing customers who may have a genuine reason to purchase another service.

After reviewing their records, the business contacts them with relevant follow-up.

Assume that 8 customers make a purchase, and each purchase generates US$250 (₦337,500) in contribution after direct delivery costs.

The resulting contribution is:

**8 × US$250 = US$2,000 (₦2,700,000).**

Now assume the business incurs US$300 (₦405,000) in additional costs to run the follow-up process, including staff time and relevant tools.

The illustrative contribution after those additional costs is:

**US$2,000 − US$300 = US$1,700 (₦2,295,000).**

These figures are hypothetical, not a forecast or a benchmark. They also do not prove that follow-up caused every purchase. Some customers might have bought without being contacted.

To evaluate the system properly, the business should compare results with a suitable baseline or, where practical, a comparable group that did not receive the intervention. It should also account for the cost of the follow-up process and any additional delivery requirements.

The commercial question is not simply whether customers purchased after receiving a message. It is whether the process created incremental value that justified its cost.

*Currency note: Naira equivalents use an illustrative reference rate of US$1 = ₦1,350, dated 14 October 2026, as specified for these examples. Exchange rates vary by source, transaction type, and date. These conversions are illustrative, not guaranteed transaction rates.*

## 8. Common Follow-Up Mistakes to Avoid

A follow-up system can create problems when it is designed around the business's desire to sell rather than the customer's situation.

### Sending the same message to everyone

Customers have different needs, purchase histories, and service requirements. Segment follow-up according to relevant differences rather than sending identical messages to the entire database.

### Contacting customers without a clear reason

Frequent communication is not inherently useful. Every message should have a purpose that is relevant to the relationship.

### Treating silence as permission to continue indefinitely

A lack of response does not necessarily mean the customer wants more messages. Establish reasonable contact limits and respect communication preferences and opt-out requests.

### Automating before fixing the process

Automation is most useful when it supports a clear workflow. Otherwise, it can reproduce errors and create additional noise.

### Measuring messages instead of outcomes

A high volume of follow-up activity can hide poor results. Track completion, response, issue resolution, renewals, repeat purchases, and commercial contribution as appropriate.

### Using follow-up to compensate for poor service

If customers are leaving because the service did not meet expectations, more messages will not solve the underlying problem. Resolve the service issue before attempting to generate another sale.

## The Bottom Line

A customer follow-up system is not a sequence of messages designed to make customers buy again. It is a process for maintaining useful relationships, fulfilling commitments, identifying relevant next steps, and learning from customer interactions.

Start with a clear reason for contacting the customer. Record the interaction, assign responsibility, choose an appropriate next action, and measure the outcome.

As the process becomes reliable, automate repetitive tasks where automation genuinely helps.

The objective is not to contact every customer more often. It is to make sure that important customer relationships do not depend entirely on memory, that genuine needs are not overlooked, and that follow-up contributes to better service and sustainable business performance.

# Late Payments Are More Than an Accounting Problem: How They Constrain Growth

A business can make a sale, deliver the work, send the invoice, and still struggle to pay its own bills.

The problem is timing. Revenue may have been earned, but until the customer pays, that money is not available to cover salaries, suppliers, rent, operating expenses, or the costs of taking on new work.

This is why late payments deserve more attention than a routine reminder from the accounts department. They can expose weaknesses in payment terms, invoicing, customer selection, internal processes, and cash-flow planning.

The goal is not simply to chase customers harder. It is to understand why money is arriving late, how the delays affect the business, and what can be changed to reduce the pressure.

## Why Late Payments Can Become a Growth Problem

Late payments affect more than the balance shown on an invoice report. They can constrain the decisions a business is able to make.

Research published by the UK government in July 2025 estimated that late payments cost the UK economy almost £11 billion annually. The research also estimated that more than 1.5 million businesses were affected each year and that affected businesses were owed approximately £26 billion at any given time. These are UK-wide estimates, not figures for Nigeria or every business market. However, they illustrate the scale that payment delays can reach in a developed business economy.

A 2025 study of 102 small and medium-sized enterprises in Cape Coast, Ghana, also found that delayed payments were a significant concern among the businesses surveyed. Most respondents reported receiving payments 60 to 90 days after invoicing, despite commonly offering 30-day credit terms. Because the study was limited to a specific location and sample, its findings should not be treated as a national estimate for Ghana, Nigeria, or other markets.

The broader lesson is relevant to service businesses: **when customers pay later than expected, the business may have to finance the gap between completing work and receiving the money owed.**

That gap can create several problems:

* **Operating pressure:** The business may have difficulty covering wages, rent, utilities, or supplier invoices while waiting for customers to pay.
* **Restricted capacity:** Money tied up in unpaid invoices may limit the business's ability to purchase materials, pay subcontractors, or accept additional projects.
* **More administrative work:** Staff must spend time checking invoice status, contacting customers, resolving disputes, and updating records.
* **Greater financial exposure:** The longer an invoice remains unpaid, the longer the business carries the risk that the customer may delay further or fail to pay.
* **Slower investment:** Planned spending on equipment, marketing, technology, or staff may need to be postponed because cash is not available when required.

Late payment does not automatically mean a business is unprofitable. It means the timing of cash inflows may be out of step with the timing of cash outflows. That mismatch can become serious even when the business is recording sales and reporting a profit.

## Revenue Is Not the Same as Cash Received

One of the most important distinctions in business finance is the difference between earning revenue and collecting payment.

Consider a service business that completes a project worth **US$2,000 (₦2.7 million)** and invoices the customer under 30-day payment terms.

The business has completed the work and raised the invoice. But if the customer does not pay until 60 days later, the business waits an additional 30 days beyond the agreed term for the money.

During that period, the business may still need to pay its employees, suppliers, subcontractors, and other operating costs.

The invoice represents money owed to the business. It is not yet money in the bank.

### An illustrative cash-flow example

Suppose a business has the following monthly figures:

| Item                                                    |                   Amount |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -----------------------: |
| Monthly operating cash outflows                         |  US$6,000 (₦8.1 million) |
| Expected customer receipts                              | US$8,000 (₦10.8 million) |
| Customer payments delayed beyond the expected date      | US$3,000 (₦4.05 million) |
| Cash available from those receipts by the expected date | US$5,000 (₦6.75 million) |
| Potential shortfall against planned outflows            | US$1,000 (₦1.35 million) |

In this simplified example, the business expects US$8,000 in customer receipts but receives only US$5,000 by the date it needs the money. If its planned cash outflows are US$6,000, it faces a potential US$1,000 shortfall.

The delayed US$3,000 has not necessarily been lost. It may still be collected later. But the business cannot use it to pay today's bills.

The figures are illustrative, not an industry benchmark or a forecast. The naira equivalents use the reference rate specified for these examples, **US$1 = ₦1,350, dated 14 October 2026**. Actual exchange rates vary, and the figures should not be used as guaranteed transaction rates.

The practical question is not simply, “How much money do customers owe us?”

It is also, “How much cash are we likely to receive, and when will it arrive compared with the payments we must make?”

## Four Questions to Ask When Payments Are Late

Before changing payment terms or introducing new collection procedures, identify where the problem is occurring.

### 1. Are Customers Paying Later Than the Agreed Terms?

Start by comparing each invoice's due date with its actual payment date.

If customers are consistently paying after the agreed deadline, the business needs to understand how widespread the delay is and whether it is concentrated among particular customers.

For example, a business may discover that most customers pay within the agreed period, while a small number of large accounts regularly pay 30 or 60 days late. That situation calls for a different response from one in which almost every customer pays late.

Review:

* Total outstanding invoices.
* The amount already past due.
* How many days each overdue invoice has remained unpaid.
* Which customers account for the largest outstanding balances.
* Whether the same customers repeatedly pay late.

A useful starting point is an **accounts receivable ageing report**, which groups unpaid invoices according to how long they have been outstanding. Common categories include current, 1–30 days overdue, 31–60 days overdue, 61–90 days overdue, and more than 90 days overdue. Businesses should adapt the categories to their payment terms and reporting needs.

The purpose is to identify the pattern, not merely produce another spreadsheet.

### 2. Are Invoices Being Sent Correctly and Promptly?

Not every payment delay begins with a customer deliberately paying late.

An invoice may be sent days after work is completed. It may contain incorrect details, omit a purchase order number, use the wrong billing contact, or lack supporting documents required by the customer's finance department.

These problems can delay approval and payment, even when the customer intends to pay.

Review the process from completed work to payment:

1. Is the invoice issued as soon as the agreed billing trigger is reached?
2. Does it contain the correct customer, amount, payment terms, and required references?
3. Has it reached the person or department responsible for processing it?
4. Does the business know whether the invoice has been received and accepted?
5. Is there a clear process for resolving disputes or requests for corrections?

If invoices are frequently disputed or returned for correction, sending more reminders will not fix the underlying problem.

The process itself needs attention.

### 3. Are Payment Terms Suitable for the Business?

A business may agree to payment terms that make a sale easier to secure but create cash-flow pressure later.

For example, a service provider might be required to pay for materials and subcontractors before starting a project, while the customer is permitted to pay 60 days after completion.

The provider is effectively financing part of the project until the customer pays.

This arrangement may be manageable for a business with sufficient cash reserves. It may be risky for a business that depends on each project payment to fund the next one.

Review whether the terms reflect the actual cost and timing of delivery.

Depending on the type of service and the agreement, possible options include:

* Requiring a deposit before work begins.
* Using milestone-based payments for longer projects.
* Billing at agreed stages rather than waiting until all work is complete.
* Agreeing on clear payment deadlines before work starts.
* Setting credit limits or reviewing terms for customers with repeated payment problems.
* Requiring approval for additional work that changes the original cost or schedule.

These options will not suit every business or customer relationship. Deposits and milestone payments must be commercially reasonable, clearly documented, and consistent with applicable contractual and legal requirements.

The aim is not to make payment terms unnecessarily difficult. It is to avoid accepting obligations that the business cannot comfortably finance.

### 4. Is the Business Following Up Consistently?

A payment process should not depend entirely on someone remembering to check an invoice.

When responsibility is unclear, an invoice may remain overdue for weeks before anyone contacts the customer. The customer may have overlooked it, be waiting for internal approval, or have a genuine dispute that has not been resolved.

A practical follow-up process defines:

* Who checks invoice status.
* When the customer is contacted before or after the due date.
* How the contact and the customer's response are recorded.
* Who resolves disputes.
* When an overdue account is escalated.
* Who decides whether further work should continue while a material balance remains unpaid.

A reminder schedule should reflect the business's payment terms, customer relationships, contract conditions, and the seriousness of the overdue balance. There is no single schedule that is right for every business.

Communication should remain professional and documented. If the customer disputes an invoice, the business should establish the reason and work toward a resolution rather than treating every delay as simple refusal to pay.

## Measure the Payment Problem Before Choosing a Solution

Once the business understands the process, it needs a small set of measures that reveal whether the situation is improving.

| Measure                     | What it helps you understand                                      |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Total accounts receivable   | How much money customers still owe                                |
| Overdue accounts receivable | How much of that money has passed its due date                    |
| Average collection time     | How long the business typically takes to collect payment          |
| Invoice dispute rate        | How often invoices are challenged or returned for correction      |
| On-time payment rate        | What proportion of invoices are paid by the agreed date           |
| Cash-flow forecast accuracy | Whether expected receipts and payments are occurring when planned |
| Time spent chasing payments | How much staff time the collection process consumes               |

Average collection time can be useful, but it should not be interpreted in isolation. It may be affected by sales growth, seasonal patterns, customer mix, and payment terms. Compare the measure over time and alongside the business's actual contractual terms.

For many service businesses, the most useful first step is simpler: create a reliable list of outstanding invoices, their due dates, the next action required, and the person responsible.

That gives the business a practical view of the problem before it invests in more software or introduces a more complicated process.

## What Can a Business Do to Reduce Payment Delays?

The right response depends on the cause. Start with the process that is creating the delay rather than introducing every possible solution at once.

### Establish clear payment expectations

Explain the price, billing milestones, payment deadline, accepted payment methods, and process for raising a dispute before work begins.

Avoid relying on informal assumptions about when the customer will pay. Record the agreed terms and make sure both parties understand them.

### Invoice promptly and accurately

Set a clear trigger for issuing invoices and assign responsibility for completing the task. Use a consistent invoice checklist to reduce missing information and avoidable corrections.

Where customers require purchase orders, acceptance documents, or other supporting information, identify those requirements before the invoice is due to be issued.

### Make payment easier to complete

Provide accurate payment instructions and make it clear who the customer should contact with questions. If a customer reports a problem, respond promptly and keep a record of the resolution.

The objective is to remove unnecessary friction while maintaining appropriate financial controls.

### Use reminders and escalation rules

Schedule invoice reviews and reminders so that overdue balances do not depend on memory. Define when an account should be escalated and who has authority to make decisions about disputed invoices or additional work.

Automation may help with reminders, recordkeeping, and reporting, but only after the underlying process is clear. Automating a confusing process can make the confusion happen faster.

### Plan for the timing gap

Even with a good payment process, some delays will occur. Build a cash-flow forecast that compares expected receipts with the timing of essential payments.

The forecast should distinguish money already received from money expected but not yet collected. It should also account for uncertainty, including customers who have a history of paying late.

Where possible, maintain an appropriate cash buffer and avoid committing all expected receipts to expenses before the money arrives.

If a shortfall is likely, address it early. Depending on the circumstances, the business may need to adjust spending, renegotiate supplier timing, arrange suitable financing, or review whether a new project can be delivered without placing existing obligations at risk. Financing has costs and conditions, so it should not be treated as an automatic solution to a recurring collection problem.

## Do Not Confuse Faster Collections With Higher Profit

Improving collections can make cash available sooner, but it does not automatically make the underlying business more profitable.

If a customer owes US$5,000 (₦6.75 million), collecting that amount may improve the cash position. It does not create US$5,000 of new revenue at the point of collection if the revenue was already recognised.

Similarly, a business may collect invoices faster and still struggle because its prices are too low, its delivery costs are too high, or its payment obligations arrive before customer receipts.

That distinction matters.

A payment delay may be the main problem, or it may be one part of a wider issue involving pricing, contract design, delivery costs, customer selection, and cash-flow planning.

The business should investigate the evidence before deciding which problem to fix first.

## A Practical Review You Can Start This Week

Set aside time to review the current position using actual business records.

**Step 1: List all unpaid invoices.**
Record the customer, invoice amount, invoice date, due date, amount outstanding, and current status.

**Step 2: Identify the overdue balances.**
Group invoices by how long they have been overdue. Highlight large balances and repeat delays.

**Step 3: Find the reason for each significant delay.**
Record whether the invoice has not been received, is awaiting approval, contains an error, is disputed, or is simply unpaid after the due date. Do not assume the cause without checking.

**Step 4: Assign the next action.**
Give each material overdue invoice an owner, a specific next step, and a review date.

**Step 5: Compare expected receipts with upcoming payments.**
Use realistic collection dates rather than assuming every overdue invoice will be paid immediately.

**Step 6: Look for recurring causes.**
If the same errors, customers, terms, or handoff problems keep appearing, investigate the process behind them.

**Step 7: Review whether the changes work.**
Track overdue balances, collection timing, disputes, and time spent chasing payment. Use the results to decide what to adjust next.

This review does not require a complicated financial system. A well-maintained spreadsheet may be enough for a small business with a manageable number of invoices. More complex operations may need accounting software, a CRM, or workflow automation, depending on where the records and responsibilities sit.

The technology should support the process, not substitute for it.

## The Bigger Question: Where Is Cash Getting Stuck?

Late payments can look like an accounts problem while revealing a wider business constraint.

Perhaps invoices are sent too late. Perhaps customers do not understand the terms. Perhaps the business accepts projects that require more upfront funding than it can provide. Perhaps responsibility for follow-up is unclear. Or perhaps the business is growing in sales without planning for the working capital required to deliver that growth.

Each situation calls for a different response.

The objective is not simply to send more reminders or pressure every customer to pay sooner. It is to identify where cash is getting stuck, understand why, and improve the process without damaging valuable customer relationships or creating new operational problems.
